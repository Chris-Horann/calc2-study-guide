Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

Differentiating asks: how fast is this function changing? Antidifferentiating asks the reverse: which function changes at this rate? Every rule on notes p. 1 is a derivative rule read backwards.

There's never just one answer. Adding a constant to a function doesn't change its slope anywhere, so if $F$ works, so does $F+5$, $F-2$, or $F+C$ for any constant $C$. An indefinite integral names that whole family: $\int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+C$.

Substitution is the chain rule run backwards. When an integrand is “something's derivative × a function of that something,” you rename the inside $u$ and the integral collapses to a basic rule.

An everyday version

A car's speedometer tells you how fast the odometer is changing at every moment. That's enough to know how far you drive, but not what the odometer read when you started. That unknown starting reading is the $+C$. Two cars with identical speedometer histories have odometers that differ by a constant.

Three questions to ask every time
  1. Can I rewrite it first? Split a fraction with a one-term denominator, and turn roots into powers (notes p. 2).
  2. Is there an inner function whose derivative is also here, up to a constant? Then $u=$ that inner function (notes pp. 3–6).
  3. Did I finish? Convert back to $x$, write $+C$, and check by differentiating.

Why $+C$, and only $+C$?

If $F'=f$ and $G'=f$ on an interval, then $(G-F)'=0$ there. A function with slope 0 everywhere on an interval is constant, so $G=F+C$.

Picture it: two curves with the same slope at every $x$ run parallel, one a vertical shift of the other. The explorer in Stage 3 shows exactly this.

Why does substitution work? (notes p. 3)

  1. The chain rule: $\dfrac{d}{dx}F\big(g(x)\big)=F'\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)$.
  2. Read it backwards. If $F'=f$, then $\displaystyle\int f\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)\,dx=F\big(g(x)\big)+C$.
  3. Name the inner function $u=g(x)$. Then $du=g'(x)\,dx$, and the integral reads $\displaystyle\int f(u)\,du=F(u)+C$: a basic rule.
  4. Put $u=g(x)$ back in. Done. Substitution doesn't create a new rule; it just exposes a chain-rule pattern that was already there.

Solve for the leftover piece (notes p. 5)

For $\int 2t^7\cos(t^8+1)\,dt$: $u=t^8+1$ gives $du=8t^7\,dt$. The integrand has $2t^7\,dt$, a quarter of that, so $2t^7\,dt=\tfrac14\,du$. The integral becomes $\tfrac14\int\cos u\,du=\tfrac14\sin(t^8+1)+C$. Solve for the piece you have, not for $dt$ alone.

A stray x? Back-substitute (notes p. 6)

For $\int (x-4)^6x\,dx$ with $u=x-4$, $du=dx$, one $x$ is left over. Solve $u=x-4$ for it: $x=u+4$. Then $\int u^6(u+4)\,du=\frac{u^8}{8}+\frac{4u^7}{7}+C=\frac{(x-4)^8}{8}+\frac{4(x-4)^7}{7}+C$.

You notice…So you…
A sum of basic functions, or a fraction with a one-term denominator: $\frac{3x+\sqrt x}{x^2}$Rewrite into powers and split, then use the basic rules (notes p. 2).

Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“For $\int x^2e^{-x^3}\,dx$ I'll let $u=x^2$, because it's the first thing I see.” Then $du=2x\,dx$, which doesn't match anything, and $e^{-x^3}$ becomes $e^{-u^{3/2}}$: worse. Choose the function that sits inside another one: here $-x^3$, whose derivative $-3x^2$ matches the $x^2$ up to a constant (notes p. 6).

Try a choice of $u$ for $\displaystyle\int x^2e^{-x^3}\,dx$ (notes p. 6). What happens to the integral?
$du=2x\,dx$, but the integrand has $x^2\,dx$. Writing $x=\sqrt u$ turns it into $\tfrac12\int\sqrt u\,e^{-u^{3/2}}\,du$, which is no easier. The chosen $u$ wasn't inside anything.
$du=-3x^2\,dx$, so $x^2\,dx=-\tfrac13\,du$ and the integral is $-\tfrac13\int e^u\,du=-\tfrac13e^u+C=-\tfrac13e^{-x^3}+C$. Here $-x^3$ is the inner function of $e^{(\cdot)}$, and its derivative is right there.
$du=-3x^2e^{-x^3}\,dx$ is the whole integrand times $-3$, so the integral is $-\tfrac13\int du=-\tfrac13e^{-x^3}+C$. It works too, which is worth noticing. The reliable habit is still “$u$ = the inner function.”
Try a choice of $u$ for $\displaystyle\int (x-4)^6\,x\,dx$ (notes p. 6).
$du=dx$, and the stray $x$ becomes $u+4$. Then $\int u^6(u+4)\,du=\int(u^7+4u^6)\,du=\frac{u^8}{8}+\frac{4u^7}{7}+C=\frac{(x-4)^8}{8}+\frac{4(x-4)^7}{7}+C$. This is the professor's solution.
$du=6(x-4)^5\,dx$. There's no $(x-4)^5$ in the integrand, so $du$ can't be formed.
$du=dx$ gives $\int(u-4)^6u\,du$: the same integral with a new letter. Renaming $x$ never simplifies anything. (Integration by parts with $u=x$ also works, as notes p. 11 shows. The answers look different but differ only by a constant.)
Stage 4 · Attempt

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

Find $\displaystyle\int t^4\sqrt{t^5+6}\,dt$ (textbook §1.1 Suggested Ex. 8). Follow the professor's five-step Key, answering each question before the explanation appears.

Step 1 · Find u
Which $u$ turns this into a basic rule?
$t^5+6$ sits inside the square root, and its derivative $5t^4$ matches the $t^4$ in front up to the constant 5.
Step 2 · du = (du/dt) dt

$\frac{d}{dt}(t^5+6)=5t^4$, so $du=5t^4\,dt$.
Step 3 · Convert to u

The integrand has $t^4\,dt$, not $5t^4\,dt$.

$t^4\,dt=\tfrac15\,du$, so the integral is $\tfrac15\int u^{1/2}\,du$. Every $t$ is gone.
Step 4 · Integrate in u

Power rule: $\int u^{1/2}\,du=\frac{u^{3/2}}{3/2}=\tfrac23u^{3/2}$. Times $\tfrac15$: $\tfrac{2}{15}u^{3/2}+C$.
Step 5 · Convert back

$\tfrac{2}{15}(t^5+6)^{3/2}+C$.
Step 6 · Check
Differentiate $\tfrac{2}{15}(t^5+6)^{3/2}$. What do you get?
The chain rule puts back exactly the $5t^4$ that substitution removed, and the constants cancel to 1. Differentiating is the fastest way to check any antiderivative.
Takeaway. Look for the function inside another, check that its derivative is present up to a constant, solve for the piece you have ($t^4\,dt=\tfrac15du$), integrate, convert back, add $+C$, and differentiate to check.
Find $\displaystyle\int (11+\sin x)^{3/8}\cos x\,dx.$
  1. Which part of the integrand sits inside another function?
  2. What's the derivative of that inside part? Is it in the integrand?
  3. How does the power rule treat $u^{3/8}$?
The $\tfrac38$ power is applied to $11+\sin x$. Try $u=11+\sin x$.
$du=\cos x\,dx$, which is exactly the rest of the integrand. The integral becomes $\int u^{3/8}\,du$.
$\int u^{3/8}\,du=\dfrac{u^{11/8}}{11/8}$. Now convert back.
  1. $u=11+\sin x$, so $du=\cos x\,dx$.
  2. $\displaystyle\int u^{3/8}\,du=\frac{u^{11/8}}{11/8}+C=\frac{8}{11}u^{11/8}+C.$
  3. Convert back: $\dfrac{8}{11}(11+\sin x)^{11/8}+C$.

Check: $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{8}{11}(11+\sin x)^{11/8}=\frac{8}{11}\cdot\frac{11}{8}(11+\sin x)^{3/8}\cos x$ ✓.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Dropping the constant factor that comes from $du$

Why it happens
$du$ usually matches the integrand only “up to a constant,” and that constant is easy to lose between lines.

Prevent it
Write the leftover piece explicitly, as the professor does: $t^7\,dt=\tfrac18du$, $x^2\,dx=-\tfrac13du$. Then differentiate your final answer.

Using the power rule on $\frac1x$

Why it happens
The power rule works for every other exponent, so $x^{-1}$ looks like just another case.

Prevent it
$\frac{x^0}{0}$ divides by zero, which is your warning. $\int\frac1x\,dx=\ln|x|+C$ is its own rule (textbook Remark 1.1.5).

Writing $\int\frac{1}{\text{anything}}\,dx=\ln|\text{anything}|+C$

Why it happens
It looks like the $\frac1x$ rule. But $\frac{d}{dx}\ln|g(x)|=\frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}$: the chain rule adds a $g'(x)$ (textbook Remark 1.1.5).

Prevent it
Only a numerator that is (a multiple of) the denominator's derivative gives a log. $\int\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\tan^{-1}x+C$, not $\ln(1+x^2)$.

Leaving a stray $x$, or not converting back

Why it happens
Mid-substitution you're juggling two letters.

Prevent it
Before integrating, check that only $u$ appears (solve for a stray $x$ if needed). After integrating, replace $u$ everywhere (step 5 of the Key).

Forgetting $+C$ or the absolute value in $\ln|x|$

Why it happens
Definite integrals don't need $+C$, and $\ln x$ looks fine for positive $x$.

Prevent it
An indefinite integral is a family, so it needs $+C$. The absolute value makes $\ln|x|$ an antiderivative of $\frac1x$ for negative $x$ too.

Stage 6 · Practice

Practice: standard applications

Find $\displaystyle\int \frac{3x+\sqrt x}{x^2}\,dx.$ Rewrite first.
Split the fraction: $\dfrac{3x}{x^2}+\dfrac{x^{1/2}}{x^2}=\dfrac{3}{x}+x^{-3/2}$.
$\int\frac3x\,dx=3\ln|x|$ and $\int x^{-3/2}\,dx=\frac{x^{-1/2}}{-1/2}$.
$3\ln|x|-2x^{-1/2}+C$, as on notes p. 2. The rewrite did all the work; no substitution was needed.
Find $\displaystyle\int\Big(\frac5w-7e^w+6\sqrt[3]{w}\Big)\,dw.$
Three basic rules, one per term. Write $\sqrt[3]w=w^{1/3}$.
$\int w^{1/3}\,dw=\frac{w^{4/3}}{4/3}=\frac34w^{4/3}$, so $6\cdot\frac34=\frac92$.
$5\ln|w|-7e^w+\tfrac92w^{4/3}+C$.
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{e^{5t}}{e^{5t}+13}\,dt.$
The numerator is (up to a constant) the derivative of the denominator. Let $u$ be the denominator.
$u=e^{5t}+13$, $du=5e^{5t}\,dt$, so $e^{5t}\,dt=\tfrac15du$ and the integral is $\tfrac15\int\frac{du}{u}$.
$\tfrac15\ln|e^{5t}+13|+C$ (notes p. 5). Since $e^{5t}+13>0$, the absolute value can also be dropped.
Find $\displaystyle\int (5w-3)^{100}\,dw.$ (Expanding the power is not an option.)
$u=5w-3$, $du=5\,dw$, so $dw=\tfrac15du$.
$\tfrac15\int u^{100}\,du=\tfrac15\cdot\frac{u^{101}}{101}$.
$\dfrac{(5w-3)^{101}}{505}+C$.
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{5}{x\ln x}\,dx.$
Which function has derivative $\frac1x$? It's sitting in the denominator.
$u=\ln x$, $du=\frac{dx}{x}$. The integral becomes $5\int\frac{du}{u}$.
$5\ln|u|+C=5\ln|\ln x|+C$.
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{e^{1/x}}{6x^2}\,dx.$
What's inside the exponential? What's its derivative?
$u=\frac1x$, $du=-\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$, so $\frac{dx}{x^2}=-du$.
$\frac16\int e^u(-du)=-\frac16e^u+C=-\frac16e^{1/x}+C$. Mind the minus sign from $du$.
Find $\displaystyle\int\tan y\,\sec^2y\,dy.$
$\sec^2y$ is the derivative of $\tan y$.
$u=\tan y$, $du=\sec^2y\,dy$: the integral is $\int u\,du$.
$\frac{u^2}{2}+C=\frac12\tan^2y+C$. Choosing $u=\sec y$ instead gives $\frac12\sec^2y+C$. Since $\sec^2y=1+\tan^2y$, the two answers differ by $\frac12$, which is absorbed into $C$. Different-looking answers can both be right (compare notes p. 21).
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_4^9\frac{e^{\sqrt x}}{\sqrt x}\,dx.$
$u=\sqrt x$, $du=\frac{dx}{2\sqrt x}$, so $\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}=2\,du$.
Change the limits: $x=4\to u=2$, $x=9\to u=3$ (or find the antiderivative $2e^{\sqrt x}$ and use the $x$-limits, as notes p. 22 does).
$2\int_2^3e^u\,du=2(e^3-e^2)\approx25.393$. Definite substitution itself is covered in §2.4.

Explain why (Level 7)

In your own words: why does $u$-substitution work, and why is there a $\tfrac14$ in $\int 2t^7\cos(t^8+1)\,dt=\tfrac14\sin(t^8+1)+C$?

A model explanation

By the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))=f(g(x))\,g'(x)$ when $F'=f$. So an integrand of the form $f(g(x))\,g'(x)$ is the derivative of $F(g(x))$, and its antiderivative is $F(g(x))+C$. Writing $u=g(x)$ and $du=g'(x)\,dx$ just renames that pattern so it looks like a basic rule. In the example, the inside $t^8+1$ has derivative $8t^7$, but only $2t^7$ is present, a quarter of it: $2t^7\,dt=\tfrac14du$. So the answer is $\tfrac14\sin(t^8+1)+C$, which differentiates back to exactly $2t^7\cos(t^8+1)$.
