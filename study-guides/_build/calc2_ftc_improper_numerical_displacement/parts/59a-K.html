Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

Density says how tightly mass is packed. If it's the same everywhere, mass is just density × size: $m=\delta V$ (3.7–3.9 notes p. 1). For a thin wire the “size” is a length; for a flat metal plate it's an area.

If the density changes from place to place, $m=\delta V$ fails for the whole object, but it still works on a piece small enough that the density is nearly constant. So cut the object into thin pieces, find each piece's mass as density × size, and add them up. In the limit that sum is an integral: $M=\int_a^b\delta(x)\,dx$ for a wire. It's the Riemann-sum idea from area and volume (§2.6, §3.5) a third time.

What to notice: on the left, the wire's density $3x^2$ grows to the right, so the right-hand pieces are darker and heavier even though every piece has the same length $\Delta x$. On the right, the plate (notes p. 5) is cut into vertical strips. Each strip has its own density $\delta(x_i)$ and its own height $f-g$, so its mass is $\delta(x_i)\cdot[f(x_i)-g(x_i)]\cdot\Delta x$.
An everyday version

A fishing rod or a baseball bat is thick at one end and thin at the other. Per centimetre, the thick end holds more mass. To weigh it in your head, you'd go along it a centimetre at a time: “this bit is about so heavy, this next bit a little lighter…” and add up. That running total is the integral, and the point where half the mass is behind you is the mass midpoint.

Three questions to ask every time
  1. What is the size of one piece? A length $\Delta x$ for a wire. An area $[f(x)-g(x)]\,\Delta x$ for a plate (top minus bottom, times the width).
  2. What is the density on that piece? $\delta(x_i)$, which depends only on $x$, so each piece or strip has one density (notes p. 3).
  3. Where does the object start and end? Those are the limits: the ends of the wire, or where the plate's curves meet.

From pieces to an integral (3.7–3.9 notes pp. 1, 3)

$$\text{wire: }\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(x_i)\,\Delta x\ \longrightarrow\ \int_a^b\delta(x)\,dx$$ $$\text{plate: }\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(x_i)\,\Delta x\,[f(x_i)-g(x_i)]\ \longrightarrow\ \int_a^b\delta(x)\,[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx$$

Subdivide $[a,b]$ into $n$ pieces of width $\Delta x=\frac{b-a}n$. The professor's key sentence: “$\delta(x)\approx$ constant on each subinterval”. On a piece where the density is constant, mass = density × size is exact, so the only error comes from the density changing a little across a piece, and that vanishes as $n\to\infty$.

Why a plate's density has to depend only on $x$

A vertical strip runs from $g(x)$ up to $f(x)$. If the density depended on $y$ too, different parts of the same strip would have different densities, and “density × strip area” wouldn't be right even for a thin strip. The notes avoid this by assuming “density only depends on $x$” (p. 3). Then every point of the strip at $x$ has density $\delta(x)$, and the strip's mass is $\delta(x)$ times its area, $[f(x)-g(x)]\,\Delta x$.

The units check this too. An area density is mass per area (kg/m²). Times an area (m²) it gives kg. A length density (kg/m) times a length (m) gives kg. The professor's green rule on p. 1: “If the question includes units, your answer needs to include units as well.”

Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“Average the density at the two ends and multiply by the length.” For $\delta=3x^2$ on $[0,2]$ that gives $\frac{0+12}{2}\cdot2=12$, but the mass is $8$. Averaging the endpoints works only when the density is a straight line (that's the Trapezoid Rule with $n=1$, §2.6). The integral is the honest average of the density, times the length.

A thin wire lies on $0\le x\le2$ with density $\delta(x)=3x^2$ (notes p. 2). Which number is its mass?
Each tiny piece has mass $3x^2\,dx$, and the integral adds them: $x^3\big|_0^2=8$.
That uses the densest value, at the right end, for the whole wire. Most of the wire is much lighter than that.
Averaging the two ends assumes the density grows in a straight line. $3x^2$ curves upward, so the endpoint average overestimates. (It's the Trapezoid Rule with one strip, §2.6.)
That's the density at the middle times the length: the Midpoint Rule with one strip. Because $3x^2$ curves upward, it underestimates. Close, but not the mass.
The plate between $y=x-x^2$ (top) and $y=-x$ (bottom) has area density $\delta(x)=x$ (notes p. 5). What is the mass of the thin strip at $x$, of width $\Delta x$?
Density × height × width, with height $=(x-x^2)-(-x)=2x-x^2$. Integrating from 0 to 2 gives $M=\frac43$.
That's only the top curve. The strip runs down to $y=-x$, so its height is $(x-x^2)-(-x)$. With this height the “mass” comes out negative ($-\frac43$), which is impossible.
That's the strip's area, with no density. Integrated, it gives the plate's area. (Here the area happens to be $\frac43$ too, a coincidence explained in the guided example below.)
For the wire with $\delta(x)=3x^2$ on $[0,2]$, the mass midpoint is $\sqrt[3]4\approx1.59$, not $1$. Why is it right of the middle?
Only $\int_0^1 3x^2\,dx=1$ of the $8$ units lie left of $x=1$. You have to go further right to collect $4$.
Not always: for $\delta=e^{-x}$ (textbook Example 3.7.6) the dense part is on the left, and the mass midpoint is far left of the middle (0.675 on $[0,4]$).
Integration doesn't shift anything. For a constant density the mass midpoint is exactly the middle.

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

A plate is bounded by the curves $f(x)=x-x^2$ and $g(x)=-x$, with area density $\delta(x)=x$. Find the mass of the plate. This is 3.7–3.9 notes p. 5 (and textbook §3.7 Suggested Ex. 5). Work it as the notes do.

Step 1 · Limits

Set $x-x^2=-x$: $x^2-2x=0$, so $x(x-2)=0$. The plate runs from $x=0$ to $x=$

$x=0$ or $x=2$. The curves meet at $(0,0)$ and $(2,-2)$.
Step 2 · Which curve is on top?
The notes test a point between the intersections. At $x=1$: $x-x^2=0$ and $-x=-1$. So…
Notes p. 5: “$x=1\to x-x^2=0,\ -x=-1$”. The parabola is on top, even though part of the plate is below the $x$-axis.
Step 3 · Strip height

$(x-x^2)-(-x)=2x-x^2$, positive on $(0,2)$ even where the strip is below the axis.
Step 4 · Mass of one strip

density × height × width $=x\,(2x-x^2)\,\Delta x$.
Step 5 · Integrate

$\big(\frac23x^3-\frac14x^4\big)\Big|_0^2=\frac{16}3-4=\frac43$ (verified by hand, SymPy and Wolfram). A coincidence worth noticing: the plate's area, $\int_0^2(2x-x^2)\,dx$, is also $\frac43$. That's because the strip heights $2x-x^2$ are symmetric about $x=1$, so weighting each strip by $\delta=x$ averages out to weighting it by 1. (§3.8 makes this precise: the plate's centroid is at $x=1$.) Don't let it fool you into dropping $\delta$: with any other density the two differ.
Step 6 · Sanity check
Part of the plate lies below the $x$-axis. Did the mass integral need to be split where the plate crosses the axis ($x=1$)?
Mass is always positive. A negative answer is a red flag for bottom-minus-top (Step 3).
Takeaway. Intersections give the limits; a test point gives the top curve. Each strip's mass is density × height × width, $\delta(x)[f(x)-g(x)]\Delta x$. Integrate over the plate's $x$-range. The plate's position relative to the $x$-axis doesn't matter.
A thin metal plate occupies the triangular region bounded by the coordinate axes and the line $y=2x-6$. Its area density is $\delta(x)=x^2+1$ kg/m². Find the mass of the plate.
  1. Sketch the triangle. Where does $y=2x-6$ cross each axis? Is the triangle above or below the $x$-axis?
  2. Which curve is the top of a vertical strip, and which is the bottom?
  3. Write density × height and integrate over the triangle's $x$-range.
The line meets the $x$-axis at $x=3$ and the $y$-axis at $y=-6$. The triangle is below the $x$-axis, for $0\le x\le3$.
Top $y=0$, bottom $y=2x-6$, so the height is $0-(2x-6)=6-2x$.
$M=\int_0^3(x^2+1)(6-2x)\,dx=\int_0^3\big(6x^2-2x^3+6-2x\big)\,dx$.

$M=\Big[2x^3-\frac{x^4}2+6x-x^2\Big]_0^3=54-40.5+18-9=\frac{45}2=22.5$ kg.

Check: the triangle's area is $\frac12\cdot3\cdot6=9$ m², and the density ranges from 1 to 10 kg/m². So the mass must be between 9 and 90 kg. Since the dense part (near $x=3$) is the narrow tip, it should be toward the low end, and $22.5$ is.

Stage 5 · Compare

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

✗ Tempting
“Mass = density × length.” For the wire on $[0,2]$ with $\delta=3x^2$: $3x^2\cdot2=6x^2$, or, plugging in some $x$, a single number like $24$.
✓ Correct
$m=\delta V$ only holds for a constant density. Here it holds on each tiny piece, so $M=\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx=8$ (notes p. 2).
Clue for next time: if the density has an $x$ in it, the answer can't have an $x$ in it, and it can't be density × length. Cut into pieces and integrate.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Density × length when the density varies

Why it happens
$m=\delta V$ is the formula you know from physics, and it's the first line of the notes.

Prevent it
Read the rest of that line: “if density is constant”. If $\delta$ depends on $x$, cut into pieces and integrate.

Forgetting the strip height for a plate

Why it happens
The wire formula $\int\delta\,dx$ is shorter, and it's the one you learned first.

Prevent it
A plate's strip is an area, not a length. Write its mass in words first: density × height × width.

Forgetting the density for a plate

Why it happens
$\int(f-g)\,dx$ is the familiar area-between-curves integral.

Prevent it
That's the plate's area, not its mass. Check: the units of your integrand should be mass per unit $x$ (kg/m), not length.

Bottom minus top, or splitting at the $x$-axis

Why it happens
Regions below the axis feel “negative” from signed area (§2.4).

Prevent it
Find the intersections, test one point between them (notes p. 5 tests $x=1$), and write top minus bottom. A strip's mass doesn't care where the axis is.

The mass midpoint as the middle of the object

Why it happens
“Midpoint” sounds like half the length.

Prevent it
It's the point with half the mass on each side: solve $\int_a^{x_m}\delta\,dx=\frac12M$. It equals the middle only when the density is constant.

The wrong lower limit for the mass midpoint

Why it happens
Most examples start at $x=0$.

Prevent it
The lower limit is where the object starts: $a=3$ on notes p. 9.

Leaving off the units

Why it happens
The integral produces a bare number.

Prevent it
The professor's green note on p. 1: if the question has units, the answer must too. Density in kg/m times length in m gives kg.

Practice: standard applications

Professor's examples to redo, textbook exercises, and new variants. Where a problem has units, you may type them (e.g. 8 kg); the checker reads the number either way.

A thin wire lies on $0\le x\le2$ with density $\delta(x)=3x^2$. (1) Find the mass of the wire. (2) Find the mass midpoint $x_m$.
$M=x^3\big|_0^2$.
$\frac12M=4=\int_0^{x_m}3x^2\,dx=x_m^3$.
(1) $M=x^3\big|_0^2=8$. (2) $x_m^3=4$, so $x_m=\sqrt[3]4\approx1.5874$, as on notes p. 2.
A plate is enclosed by the curves $y=x^2$ and $y=2-x^2$, with area density $\delta(x)=3^x$. Find an integral that gives the mass of the plate. Set up only.
$x^2=2-x^2$ gives $2x^2=2$, so $x=\pm1$.
At $x=0$, $2-x^2=2$ is above $x^2=0$. Height $=(2-x^2)-x^2$.

$M=\int_{-1}^{1}3^x\big(2-x^2-x^2\big)\,dx=\int_{-1}^{1}3^x\big(2-2x^2\big)\,dx$, as on notes p. 4. The notes stop here.

If you want the number: integration by parts twice gives $M=\frac{8(5\ln3-4)}{3\ln^3 3}\approx3.0027$ (checked with SymPy, mpmath and Wolfram). The plate's area is $\frac83\approx2.667$; the mass is a bit bigger because the dense right half outweighs the light left half.

A wire is stretched along the $x$-axis from $x=0$ to $x=4$ feet. Its density is $\delta(x)=e^{-x}$ slugs/ft. Find the mass of the left half ($0\le x\le2$), the mass of the right half ($2\le x\le4$), and the mass midpoint $x_m$.
$\int e^{-x}\,dx=-e^{-x}+C$ (substitute $u=-x$).
For $x_m$: set $\int_0^{x_m}e^{-x}\,dx=\int_{x_m}^4e^{-x}\,dx$, so $1-e^{-x_m}=e^{-x_m}-e^{-4}$.
Left: $1-e^{-2}\approx0.8647$ slugs. Right: $e^{-2}-e^{-4}\approx0.1170$ slugs. Midpoint: $2e^{-x_m}=1+e^{-4}$, so $x_m=\ln\frac{2}{1+e^{-4}}\approx0.6750$ ft. About 88% of the mass is in the left half, a bit more than seven-eighths.
The density of a thin wire lying on $0\le x\le4$ is $\delta(x)=x+7$. Find (a) the total mass and (b) the mass midpoint.
$M=\Big[\frac{x^2}2+7x\Big]_0^4$.
$\frac{x_m^2}2+7x_m=18$ is a quadratic: $x_m^2+14x_m-36=0$. Keep the root that lies on the wire.
(a) $8+28=36$. (b) $x_m=\frac{-14+\sqrt{196+144}}{2}=-7+\sqrt{85}\approx2.2195$. The other root, $-7-\sqrt{85}$, isn't on the wire. The density only changes from 7 to 11, so the midpoint is just a little right of 2.
The density of a thin wire lying on $0\le x\le\frac\pi2$ is $\delta(x)=\sin x$. Find (a) the total mass and (b) the mass midpoint.
$M=-\cos x\Big|_0^{\pi/2}$.
$\int_0^{x_m}\sin x\,dx=1-\cos x_m=\frac12$.
(a) $M=0-(-1)=1$. (b) $\cos x_m=\frac12$, so $x_m=\frac\pi3\approx1.0472$, since $\cos\frac\pi3=\frac12$.
A thin plate is the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(0,2)$ and $(5,0)$, with area density $\delta(x)=cx^2$ kg/m². Its total mass is 25 kg. Find the strip height and the constant $c$.
The hypotenuse goes through $(0,2)$ and $(5,0)$: $y=2-\frac25x$. The bottom is the $x$-axis.
$\int_0^5cx^2\big(2-\frac25x\big)\,dx=c\Big[\frac23x^3-\frac1{10}x^4\Big]_0^5$.
$c\big(\frac{250}3-\frac{625}{10}\big)=c\cdot\frac{125}6=25$, so $c=\frac65=1.2$ kg/m⁴ (the units make $cx^2$ come out in kg/m²).
The density of a thin wire lying on $1\le x\le2$ is $\delta(x)=\dfrac{4}{9-x^2}$. Find its total mass.
Factor: $9-x^2=(3-x)(3+x)$. Write $\frac{4}{(3-x)(3+x)}=\frac{A}{3-x}+\frac{B}{3+x}$.
$A=B=\frac23$. Then $\int\frac{dx}{3-x}=-\ln|3-x|$ and $\int\frac{dx}{3+x}=\ln|3+x|$.
$M=\frac23\Big[\ln\Big|\frac{3+x}{3-x}\Big|\Big]_1^2=\frac23\big(\ln5-\ln2\big)=\frac23\ln\frac52\approx0.6109$.
The density of a thin wire lying on $3\le x\le9$ is $\delta(x)=x\ln x$. Find its total mass.
By parts with $u=\ln x$ (LIATE), $dv=x\,dx$: $\int x\ln x\,dx=\frac{x^2}2\ln x-\frac{x^2}4+C$.
At $x=9$: $\frac{81}2\ln9-\frac{81}4$. At $x=3$: $\frac92\ln3-\frac94$. Use $\ln9=2\ln3$.
$M=\big(81\ln3-\frac{81}4\big)-\big(\frac92\ln3-\frac94\big)=\frac{153}2\ln3-18\approx66.044$.

A wire on $[0,10]$ has density $\delta(x)=ce^x$, where $c>0$ is a constant you aren't told. Find the mass midpoint.
$M=c(e^{10}-1)$. Set $\int_0^{x_m}ce^x\,dx=\frac12M$. What happens to $c$?
$c(e^{x_m}-1)=\frac c2(e^{10}-1)$, so $e^{x_m}=\frac{1+e^{10}}2$.
$c$ cancels, so it doesn't matter: $x_m=\ln\frac{1+e^{10}}2\approx9.3069$. That's about $\ln2\approx0.69$ from the right end: halving an exponential moves you back by $\ln 2$. Doubling every density (changing $c$) doubles both halves, so it can't move the midpoint.
A thin wire lies along the whole half-line $x\ge0$, with density $\delta(x)=\dfrac{1}{(1+x)^2}$ kg/m. Find (a) its total mass, or show it's infinite, and (b) its mass midpoint.
The integral is improper (Type I, §2.5). Replace $\infty$ by $b$: $\int_0^b(1+x)^{-2}\,dx$.
$\int_0^{x_m}(1+x)^{-2}\,dx=1-\frac1{1+x_m}=\frac12$.
(a) $\lim_{b\to\infty}\big(1-\frac1{1+b}\big)=1$ kg, finite even though the wire is infinitely long. (b) $\frac1{1+x_m}=\frac12$, so $x_m=1$ m: half of all the mass is in the first metre.

Explain why (Level 7)

Why isn't the mass of a wire just its density times its length? And why does a plate's mass integral need both $\delta(x)$ and $f(x)-g(x)$?

A model explanation

Mass = density × size is only true when the density is the same everywhere. If $\delta$ changes along the wire, there's no single density to multiply by. So cut the wire into short pieces of length $\Delta x$. On each piece the density is nearly constant, $\delta(x_i)$, so that piece's mass is about $\delta(x_i)\Delta x$. Adding the pieces gives a Riemann sum, and as the pieces shrink the error goes away: $M=\int_a^b\delta(x)\,dx$ (3.7–3.9 notes p. 1). For a plate, the pieces are vertical strips. Because the density depends only on $x$, a whole strip has one density $\delta(x_i)$. But a strip is an area, not a length: its height is top minus bottom, $f(x_i)-g(x_i)$, and its width is $\Delta x$. So its mass is $\delta(x_i)\,[f(x_i)-g(x_i)]\,\Delta x$, and $M=\int_a^b\delta(x)[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx$ (notes p. 3). Without $\delta$ you'd get the area; without $f-g$ you'd be treating the plate as a wire.
