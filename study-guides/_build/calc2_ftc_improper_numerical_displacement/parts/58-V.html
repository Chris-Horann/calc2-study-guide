Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

Slice the solid like a loaf of bread. If you know the area $A(x)$ of the slice at position $x$, a thin slice of thickness $\Delta x$ has volume about $A(x)\,\Delta x$. Adding the slices gives a Riemann sum, and making them thinner turns it into $V=\int_a^bA(x)\,dx$ (3.3–3.5 notes p. 5).

Spin a flat region around a line and you get a solid of revolution. Every slice perpendicular to that line is a disk, or, if the region doesn't reach the line, a washer (a disk with a hole). A washer's area is the big disk minus the hole: $\pi(R^2-r^2)$ (3.3–3.5 notes p. 7).

What to notice: the triangle from 3.3–3.5 notes p. 6 (under $y=2x$, from $x=0$ to $x=2$) spins around the $x$-axis into a cone. The thin strip at $x$ sweeps out a thin disk of radius $2x$. The cone is a stack of such disks.
An everyday version

A stack of equal coins is a cylinder: every slice has the same area $A$, so $V=A\cdot h$. A stack of shrinking coins is a cone. A stack of washers (the metal rings under a bolt) is a pipe, with the hole running all the way through.

Three questions to ask every time
  1. Which way do I slice? Always perpendicular to the axis of rotation: $dx$ for a horizontal axis, $dy$ for a vertical one.
  2. Disk or washer? Does the region reach the axis all along the slice? If not, there's a hole.
  3. What are $R$ and $r$? The distances from the axis to the far edge and the near edge of the region. For an axis like $y=-2$, measure from $y=-2$, not from the $x$-axis (the green note on 3.3–3.5 notes p. 7).

From slices to an integral (3.3–3.5 notes p. 5)

$$\Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n},\qquad V_{\text{slice }i}\approx A(x_i)\,\Delta x,\qquad V\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(x_i)\,\Delta x\ \longrightarrow\ \int_a^bA(x)\,dx$$

It's the notes p. 34 move (§2.6) again. There, strips of area $f(x_i)\,\Delta x$ add up to an area. Here, slabs of volume $A(x_i)\,\Delta x$ add up to a volume. The page shows two textbook figures: a solid cut at $x$, with face area $A(x)$, and one slab of width $\Delta x$.

Why a washer's area is $\pi(R^2-r^2)$

A washer is a disk of radius $R$ with a disk of radius $r$ cut out of its middle. Areas subtract: $\pi R^2-\pi r^2=\pi(R^2-r^2)$. When the region reaches the axis there's no hole, so $r=0$ and the slice is a disk, $\pi R^2$. That's notes p. 6; the notes never say “disk,” they just use $r=0$.

It is not $\pi(R-r)^2$. That would be one disk whose radius is the ring's width. For $R=2$ and $r=1$, the ring has area $3\pi$, but $\pi(R-r)^2=\pi$. The textbook calls this “a common, but terrible, mistake.”

What to notice
  1. With 3.3–3.5 notes p. 6: y = 2x, y = 0, x = 2, every slice is a disk: the region reaches the axis, so $r=0$. The bottom graph shows $A(x)=4\pi x^2$. The area under it, $\frac{32\pi}3\approx33.51$, is the cone formula $\frac13\pi(4)^2(2)$.
  2. With 3.3–3.5 notes p. 7: between y = 2x and y = x, the region starts at $y=x$, above the axis, so every slice has a hole: $R=2x$ (far edge) and $r=x$ (near edge). The volume is $8\pi\approx25.13$: the p. 6 cone minus an inner cone.
  3. Raise $n$. The stack of slabs, $\sum A(x_i)\,\Delta x$ with right endpoints as on notes p. 5, approaches the exact volume, just as Riemann sums approach an area.
  4. Drag the axis. For the notes p. 7 region, move it down to $y=-1$: $R$ and $r$ both grow by 1, and the volume jumps. Move it into the region and the explorer refuses, because the slices would no longer be washers.
  5. Compare Textbook Ex. 3.5.8: between y = x and y = x² with Textbook Ex. 3.5.9: same region, axis y = 2. Around $y=2$ the far edge is the lower curve, so $R=2-x^2$ and $r=2-x$. The volume grows from $\frac{2\pi}{15}$ to $\frac{8\pi}{15}$ because the region is farther from the axis.

Step 1 · Slice
How should you slice?
The notes' rule: “slice perpendicular to axis of rotation.” For the $x$-axis, that means a vertical slice at each $x$ in $[0,2]$.
Step 2 · Disk or washer?
Spun around the $x$-axis, each slice is a…
At position $x$ the region runs from $y=x$ up to $y=2x$. Spinning that segment sweeps out a ring.
Step 3 · Outer radius

The far edge is $y=2x$ and the axis is $y=0$, so $R=2x-0=2x$.
Step 4 · Inner radius

The near edge is $y=x$, so $r=x$. The green arrows on notes p. 7 mark exactly these two radii.
Step 5 · Slice area

$\pi\big((2x)^2-x^2\big)=\pi(4x^2-x^2)=3\pi x^2$.
Step 6 · Integrate

$\pi\big[x^3\big]_0^2=8\pi\approx25.13$ (verified by hand, SymPy and Wolfram).
Step 7 · Check with geometry
The solid is a cone of radius 4 and height 2 with a cone-shaped hole of radius 2 and height 2. Does $8\pi$ fit?
Two routes, one answer. When a solid turns out to be a familiar shape, check your integral against its formula.
Takeaway. Slice perpendicular to the axis. $R$ is the distance to the far edge and $r$ the distance to the near edge ($r=0$ for a disk). Then $A=\pi(R^2-r^2)$; integrate it over the region's $x$-range.
Let $S$ be the region between $y=\sqrt x$ and $y=x^2$ for $0\le x\le1$. Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating $S$ about the $x$-axis.
  1. Sketch both curves on $[0,1]$. Which one is on top?
  2. Slice perpendicular to the $x$-axis. Which edge is far from the axis ($R$), and which is near ($r$)?
  3. $A(x)=\pi(R^2-r^2)$. Integrate it from 0 to 1.
The curves meet at $x=0$ and $x=1$, and $\sqrt x\ge x^2$ in between. Try $x=\frac14$: $\frac12$ vs. $\frac1{16}$.
$R=\sqrt x$ and $r=x^2$, so $A(x)=\pi(x-x^4)$.
$\int_0^1\pi(x-x^4)\,dx=\pi\big(\frac12-\frac15\big)$.

$V=\pi\int_0^1(x-x^4)\,dx=\pi\big(\frac12-\frac15\big)=\frac{3\pi}{10}\approx0.9425$.

Sanity check: without the hole, the solid under $\sqrt x$ alone has volume $\pi\int_0^1x\,dx=\frac\pi2\approx1.571$. The hole removes $\pi\int_0^1x^4\,dx=\frac\pi5$, leaving $\frac{3\pi}{10}$ ✓.

Stage 5 · Compare

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

✗ Tempting
“A washer's area is $\pi(R-r)^2$,” so for the notes p. 7 region $V=\pi\int_0^2(2x-x)^2\,dx=\frac{8\pi}{3}$.
✓ Correct
A washer is a big disk minus a hole: $\pi R^2-\pi r^2$. So $V=\pi\int_0^2\big((2x)^2-x^2\big)\,dx=8\pi$ (3.3–3.5 notes p. 7).
Clue for next time: subtract the areas, not the radii: $\pi(R^2-r^2)$.

What went wrong

Line 3 swaps the radii. For $0<y<1$, $\sqrt y>y$ (try $y=\frac14$: $\frac12>\frac14$), so the far edge is $x=\sqrt y$: $R=\sqrt y$ and $r=y$. Then $V=\pi\int_0^1(y-y^2)\,dy=\frac\pi6$. A negative “volume” always means $R$ and $r$ are swapped.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

$\pi(R-r)^2$ instead of $\pi(R^2-r^2)$

Why it happens
$R-r$ looks like “the radius of the ring.”

Prevent it
Think big disk minus hole. Test it: $R=2$, $r=1$ gives a ring of area $3\pi$, not $\pi$.

Measuring from the $x$-axis when the axis is somewhere else

Why it happens
“$R$ = the top curve” works only for the $x$-axis.

Prevent it
Draw the axis first. $R$ and $r$ are distances to it: for $y=2$ they're $2-(\text{curve})$, and for $y=-1$ they're $(\text{curve})+1$ (notes p. 7, green note).

Swapping $R$ and $r$

Why it happens
The top curve isn't always the far one. For an axis above the region, the bottom curve is farther away.

Prevent it
$R$ is the far edge and $r$ the near edge. A negative result means they're swapped.

Ignoring the hole

Why it happens
Disks are the first case you learn.

Prevent it
Ask whether the region reaches the axis all along the slice. If it doesn't, there's a hole and $r>0$.

Integrating in $x$ when the axis is vertical

Why it happens
Habit: most problems use $dx$.

Prevent it
Slices must be perpendicular to the axis. For the $y$-axis, slice horizontally and use $R(y)$, $r(y)$, $dy$ and $y$-limits (textbook Ex. 3.5.10).

Forgetting $\pi$ or the square

Why it happens
$\int\pi R\,dx$ and $\int R^2\,dx$ both look almost right.

Prevent it
Each slice's face is a circle or a ring, so its area has a $\pi$ and a square.

Explain why (Level 7)

Why is the volume of a solid $\int_a^bA(x)\,dx$? And why is a washer's area $\pi(R^2-r^2)$ rather than $\pi(R-r)^2$?

A model explanation

Slice the solid perpendicular to the $x$-axis into thin slabs of thickness $\Delta x$. The slab at $x$ is nearly a flat plate with face area $A(x)$, so its volume is about $A(x)\,\Delta x$: face area × thickness, like a coin. Adding the slabs gives the Riemann sum $\sum A(x_i)\,\Delta x$, which becomes $\int_a^bA(x)\,dx$ as the slabs get thinner (3.3–3.5 notes p. 5). For a solid of revolution, each slab's face is a disk of radius $R$ with a disk of radius $r$ removed, so its area is $\pi R^2-\pi r^2=\pi(R^2-r^2)$. $\pi(R-r)^2$ would be the area of one disk whose radius is the ring's width, which is much smaller: for $R=2$ and $r=1$ the ring's area is $3\pi$, but $\pi(R-r)^2=\pi$.
