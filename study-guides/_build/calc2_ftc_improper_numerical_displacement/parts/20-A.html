Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

An integral is a running total of tiny contributions. Slice the region between a graph and the axis into very thin strips. Each strip contributes (height) × (width). Where the graph is above the axis the height is positive and the strip adds. Where it's below, the height is negative and the strip subtracts. Add up every strip and you get $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$, the net (signed) area.

Adding infinitely many slivers sounds impossible. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says you don't have to. If you know a function $F$ whose rate of change is $f$, the sum of all the slivers is just how much $F$ changed: $F(b)-F(a)$.

What to notice: thin strips above the axis (blue, +) add; strips below (orange, −) subtract. The integral is the total with signs.
An everyday version

Water flows into a tank at $r(t)$ liters per minute, and a negative $r$ means it's draining. Adding up $r(t)\,dt$ from $t=a$ to $t=b$ gives the net change in the water, $V(b)-V(a)$. You don't track every minute; you compare the start and end readings. That's the FTC. §3.1 is this same idea with velocity and position.

Two questions to ask every time
  1. Net or total? Does the question want signed area (the plain integral) or actual area (every piece positive)?
  2. Is $F(b)-F(a)$ allowed? Only if $f$ is continuous on the whole closed interval $[a,b]$ (notes p. 25) and you can find an antiderivative.

Prerequisite reminder (§1.1)

The FTC needs an antiderivative $F$ of $f$ (any one works). You don't write $+C$: $\big(F(b)+C\big)-\big(F(a)+C\big)=F(b)-F(a)$, so the constant always cancels (textbook Example 2.4.11).

Evaluation-bar notation: $F(x)\Big|_a^b = F(b)-F(a)$. Always wrap $F(a)$ in parentheses before subtracting, as on notes p. 25: $(8-4)-(4-1)$.

Why does subtracting two values of $F$ add up all the strips?

  1. Let $A(x)$ be the signed area from $a$ up to a moving right edge $x$. At the start there's nothing yet: $A(a)=0$.
  2. Nudge the edge from $x$ to $x+dx$. You add one thin strip of height $f(x)$ and width $dx$, so $A(x+dx)-A(x)\approx f(x)\,dx$. In other words, $A$ grows at rate $f$: $A'(x)=f(x)$.
  3. So $A$ is an antiderivative of $f$. Any antiderivative $F$ differs from it by a constant: $A(x)=F(x)+C$.
  4. Use $A(a)=0$: $0=F(a)+C$, so $C=-F(a)$. At the right end, $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = A(b) = F(b)-F(a).$

The textbook proves this as Theorem 2.4.10 and adds an important warning: the FTC is not the definition of the integral. The integral is still a continuous sum of tiny contributions; the FTC is the shortcut for computing it. That's why §2.6 (when there's no usable $F$) still makes sense.

Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“It's an area question, so I'll just compute $F(b)-F(a)$.” That returns net area: pieces below the axis subtract. It's right only if $f\ge 0$ on the whole interval.

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

Find the total area between $y=x^2-2x$ and the $x$-axis on $[0,3]$, then compare it with $\displaystyle\int_0^3 (x^2-2x)\,dx$. Answer each question before the explanation appears.

Step 1
Where can $x^2-2x$ change sign on $[0,3]$?
Factor: $x^2-2x=x(x-2)$, which is 0 at $x=0$ and $x=2$. A continuous function can only switch sign where it equals 0, so the split point inside $[0,3]$ is $x=2$.
Step 2
On $(0,2)$, is $x^2-2x$ positive or negative?
Test $x=1$: $1-2=-1<0$. So on $[0,2]$, $|x^2-2x|=-(x^2-2x)=2x-x^2$. On $[2,3]$ (test $x=3$: $3>0$) it's just $x^2-2x$.
Step 3

Compute the first piece:

$\left[x^2-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=\left(4-\tfrac{8}{3}\right)-0=\tfrac{4}{3}.$
Step 4

Compute the second piece:

$\left[\tfrac{x^3}{3}-x^2\right]_2^3=(9-9)-\left(\tfrac{8}{3}-4\right)=0-\left(-\tfrac{4}{3}\right)=\tfrac{4}{3}.$ Watch the sign: you're subtracting a negative $F(2)$.
Step 5

So the total area is:

$\tfrac{4}{3}+\tfrac{4}{3}=\tfrac{8}{3}\approx 2.667.$
Step 6
Now predict $\displaystyle\int_0^3 (x^2-2x)\,dx$ (no absolute value) before computing it.
Net $=A_{\text{above}}-A_{\text{below}}=\tfrac43-\tfrac43=0$. Check with the FTC: $\left[\tfrac{x^3}{3}-x^2\right]_0^3=9-9=0$. The same region has total area $\tfrac83$ but net area $0$.
Takeaway. The split points are the zeros of the integrand inside $[a,b]$. The FTC always returns the net value. For total area, integrate $|f|$ one sign-constant piece at a time. Notes p. 25 does exactly this for $|2x-4|$ on $[1,4]$.
Find $\displaystyle\int_0^3 \left|x^2-4\right|\,dx.$
  1. What does $|\,\cdot\,|$ do to negative values?
  2. Where is $x^2-4=0$? Is that point inside $[0,3]$?
  3. On each side of that point, what is $|x^2-4|$ without the absolute value bars?
The absolute value changes the formula where $x^2-4$ changes sign. Where is that in $[0,3]$?
$x^2-4<0$ on $(0,2)$ and $>0$ on $(2,3)$. So $|x^2-4|=4-x^2$ on $[0,2]$ and $x^2-4$ on $[2,3]$. Set up the two integrals.
$\displaystyle\int_0^2 (4-x^2)\,dx=\tfrac{16}{3}$. Now do $\displaystyle\int_2^3 (x^2-4)\,dx$ and add.
  1. Zero inside the interval: $x^2-4=0 \Rightarrow x=2$ (the root $x=-2$ is outside).
  2. $\displaystyle\int_0^2 (4-x^2)\,dx=\left[4x-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=8-\tfrac83=\tfrac{16}{3}.$
  3. $\displaystyle\int_2^3 (x^2-4)\,dx=\left[\tfrac{x^3}{3}-4x\right]_2^3=(9-12)-\left(\tfrac83-8\right)=-3+\tfrac{16}{3}=\tfrac{7}{3}.$
  4. Total: $\tfrac{16}{3}+\tfrac{7}{3}=\tfrac{23}{3}\approx 7.667.$

Sanity check: the net integral is $\int_0^3(x^2-4)\,dx=9-12=-3=\tfrac73-\tfrac{16}{3}$. Total ≥ |net| ✓.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Reporting net area when the question asks for area

Why it happens
The FTC computes signed area, and it's easy to forget that below-axis strips subtract.

Prevent it
Before integrating, sketch or sign-test $f$ on $[a,b]$. If it changes sign and the question says “area,” split and use $|f|$.

Dropping parentheses in $F(b)-F(a)$

Why it happens
$F(a)$ often has several terms, and the minus sign has to hit all of them.

Prevent it
Always write $F(b)-\big(F(a)\big)$ with parentheses, like notes p. 25: $(8-4)-(4-1)$.

Using the FTC across a blow-up

Why it happens
The limits look harmless, and the problem point sits inside the interval.

Prevent it
Scan $[a,b]$ for zero denominators, negative powers of zero, $\ln 0$, and $\tan(\pi/2)$ before you integrate. Found one? Go to §2.5.

Keeping the x-limits after substituting

Why it happens
The substitution changes the variable, but it's easy to leave the limits alone.

Prevent it
Write “$x=1\to u=4$, $x=3\to u=12$” right next to your substitution, as notes p. 26 does.

Splitting at the wrong place

Why it happens
It's easy to confuse zeros of $F$ with zeros of $f$.

Prevent it
Split where the integrand (the inside of the absolute value) is zero, because that's where the strips change sign.

Practice: standard applications

Find $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx$, the area under one hump of $\sin x$.
An antiderivative of $\sin x$ is $-\cos x$.
Compute $-\cos\pi-(-\cos 0)$. What are $\cos\pi$ and $\cos 0$?
$\big[-\cos x\big]_0^{\pi}=-\cos\pi-(-\cos0)=1+1=2$. Here $\sin x\ge0$ on $[0,\pi]$, so net area = area.
For $\sin x$ on $[0,2\pi]$, find (a) the integral $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx$ and (b) the total area between the curve and the $x$-axis.
Where is $\sin x$ negative on $[0,2\pi]$?
Split at $\pi$: $\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx=2$ and $\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx=-2$.
(a) $\big[-\cos x\big]_0^{2\pi}=-1-(-1)=0$. (b) $|2|+|-2|=4$.
For $y=x^3$ on $[-1,2]$, find (a) $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{2}x^3\,dx$ and (b) the total area between the curve and the $x$-axis.
$x^3$ changes sign at $x=0$.
$\int_{-1}^{0}x^3\,dx=-\tfrac14$ and $\int_0^2x^3\,dx=4$.
(a) $\left[\tfrac{x^4}{4}\right]_{-1}^{2}=4-\tfrac14=\tfrac{15}{4}$. (b) $\tfrac14+4=\tfrac{17}{4}$.
Find $\displaystyle\int_0^2 x\,(x^2+1)^3\,dx.$
An inner function, $x^2+1$, has its derivative $2x$ sitting nearby (up to a constant). That's the substitution clue.
$u=x^2+1$, $du=2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx=\tfrac12du$. New limits: $x=0\to u=1$ and $x=2\to u=5$.
$\tfrac12\int_1^5u^3\,du=\tfrac12\left[\tfrac{u^4}{4}\right]_1^5$.
$\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{625-1}{4}=\tfrac{624}{8}=78.$
Find $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\frac{3\sin x}{\sqrt{2-\cos x}}\,dx.$
Which inner expression has derivative $\sin x$?
$u=2-\cos x$, $du=\sin x\,dx$. Limits: $x=0\to u=1$, $x=\pi\to u=3$.
$3\int_1^3u^{-1/2}\,du=3\big[2\sqrt u\,\big]_1^3$.
$6\sqrt3-6\approx4.392$. (Is this proper? $2-\cos x\ge1$, so the denominator is never 0 ✓.)
Find $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\sin t\cos t\,dt.$ (Look for a shortcut once you change the limits.)
Try $u=\sin t$. What are the new limits?
$t=0\to u=0$ and $t=\pi\to u=\sin\pi=0$. What is $\int_0^0u\,du$?
$\int_0^0u\,du=0$. Geometrically, $\sin t\cos t=\tfrac12\sin 2t$ has equal areas above and below the axis on $[0,\pi]$. The textbook uses the same trick for an integral with no elementary antiderivative: both $u$-limits come out 0.
Find $\displaystyle\int_1^2\frac{2}{(2w-1)^2}\,dw.$ First check: is this proper?
$2w-1=0$ only at $w=\tfrac12$, outside $[1,2]$, so the FTC applies. Try $u=2w-1$.
$du=2\,dw$. Limits: $w=1\to u=1$ and $w=2\to u=3$. That leaves $\int_1^3u^{-2}\,du$.
$\left[-\tfrac1u\right]_1^3=-\tfrac13+1=\tfrac23.$

Find the total area trapped between $y=-2+\dfrac{3}{1+x^2}$ and the $x$-axis over $[-1,1]$. (The curve is sometimes above and sometimes below the axis.)
Where is $-2+\frac{3}{1+x^2}=0$? Solve $\frac{3}{1+x^2}=2$.
$x=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$. The function is positive between them and negative outside. It's even, so work on $[0,1]$ and double.
An antiderivative is $G(x)=3\arctan x-2x$ (the arctan rule, notes p. 1). Total $=2\Big[\big(G(c)-G(0)\big)-\big(G(1)-G(c)\big)\Big]$ with $c=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$.

With $c=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$: $G(c)-G(0)=3\arctan c-\sqrt2$ and $G(1)-G(c)=\big(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}-2\big)-\big(3\arctan c-\sqrt2\big)$.

Total $=2\Big[6\arctan\tfrac1{\sqrt2}-2\sqrt2-\tfrac{3\pi}{4}+2\Big]=4-4\sqrt2+12\arctan\tfrac1{\sqrt2}-\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\approx1.0165.$ Compare the net integral, $\tfrac{3\pi}{2}-4\approx0.712$.

Explain why (Level 7)

In your own words: why does $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ equal $F(b)-F(a)$, and why does $+C$ never matter in a definite integral?

A model explanation

Let $A(x)$ be the signed area accumulated from $a$ to $x$. Moving $x$ a little adds a strip of height $f(x)$, so $A$ grows at rate $f$: $A'=f$. That makes $A$ an antiderivative, so $A(x)=F(x)+C$. Since $A(a)=0$, $C=-F(a)$, and the whole integral is $A(b)=F(b)-F(a)$. Any other antiderivative is $F+C$, and $(F(b)+C)-(F(a)+C)=F(b)-F(a)$: the constant cancels.
