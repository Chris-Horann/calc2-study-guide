Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

How long is a curve? Imagine a particle running along it from one end to the other, once. The curve's length is the distance that particle travels, and you already know how to compute that: $\int|\vec v|\,dt$ (§3.3). Your notes say it in green: “View the curve as the trajectory of a moving particle with position vector $(x(t),y(t))$” (3.3–3.5 notes p. 2).

For a graph $y=f(x)$, let the particle's $x$-coordinate be the time, so its position is $(x,f(x))$. Its velocity is $(1,f'(x))$ and its speed is $\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}$. Adding up speed × time gives the arc length, $\int_a^b\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\,dx$.

What to notice: zoom in on a smooth curve and a short piece of it looks straight. That piece is the hypotenuse of a tiny right triangle with legs $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$. The integral adds up the hypotenuses.
An everyday version

To measure a winding road on a map, lay a string along it, then straighten the string and measure that. Or drive the road and read the odometer. Both measure the path itself. The straight line between the two ends is always shorter.

Three questions to ask every time
  1. How is the curve given? As $(x(t),y(t))$: use $\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}\,dt$. As $y=f(x)$: use $\sqrt{1+f'^2}\,dx$.
  2. Does the inside simplify? Square and add, then look for a perfect square, $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$, or something linear.
  3. No antiderivative? Set up the integral, then estimate it with a numerical rule (§2.6), exactly as 3.3–3.5 notes p. 4 does.

Why the formula works

  • Chop the curve into $n$ short pieces. Each piece is almost a straight segment, so by Pythagoras its length is about $\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}$. The textbook's Figure 3.14 writes this as $(ds)^2=(dx)^2+(dy)^2$.
  • Use the slope. Over a short piece, $\Delta y\approx f'(x)\,\Delta x$. So each piece has length about $\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\,\Delta x$, a Riemann-sum term like the ones on notes p. 34.
  • Add up, then let $n\to\infty$. The sum becomes $\int_a^b\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\,dx$. With a parameter $t$, the same steps give $\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}\,\Delta t$ per piece: speed × time.
  • Two built-in checks. The straight chord between the endpoints is the shortest path, so the arc length is at least the chord. And since $\sqrt{1+f'^2}\ge1$, a graph over $[a,b]$ is at least $b-a$ long.

Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“The length of $y=\sin x$ on $[0,2\pi]$ is $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx=0$.” Or: “It's $2\pi$, the distance along the axis.” The first is a signed area; the second is the straight chord. The wave is longer than both, about 7.64.

What to notice
  1. Set $n=1$: the polygon is the straight chord between the endpoints, the shortest possible path. As you raise $n$, the polygon's length grows toward the arc length and never passes it.
  2. The highlighted chord is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$. In the bottom graph its bar has height chord ÷ $\Delta x$, close to $\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}$ on that piece. The bars' total area is the polygon's length, and as $n$ grows it becomes the area under $\sqrt{1+f'^2}$: the arc length.
  3. With 3.3–3.5 notes p. 4: y = sin x on [0, 2π], choose Trapezoid Rule with $n=4$. You get $T_4=\pi(\sqrt2+1)\approx7.5845$, the notes' answer. The actual length is about 7.6404. $\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}$ has no elementary antiderivative, which is why the notes estimate.
  4. With 3.3–3.5 notes p. 3: semicircle (cos t, sin t), the bottom graph is the speed, flat at 1. The length is just the time elapsed, $\pi$.
  5. With 3.3–3.5 notes p. 3: g(x) = (2/3)(x + 4)^(3/2), even $n=1$ is within 0.01 of $\frac{74}{3}$: this curve is nearly straight on $[4,11]$.
  6. With Circle traced twice: 0 ≤ t ≤ 4π, the integral is $4\pi$, the distance the particle travels. The circle itself is only $2\pi$ long. The formula gives a curve's length only when the curve is traced once.

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

Find the length of the curve $g(x)=\tfrac23(x+4)^{3/2}$ for $4\le x\le11$ (3.3–3.5 notes p. 3). Answer each step before you reveal it.

Step 1 · Which formula?
The length is…
The curve is a graph $y=g(x)$, so use the second formula on notes p. 2.
Step 2 · Differentiate

$g'(x)=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac32(x+4)^{1/2}=(x+4)^{1/2}$ (notes p. 3). The inner derivative of $x+4$ is 1.
Step 3 · Square and add 1

Squaring undoes the square root: $1+(x+4)=x+5$. The integrand is just $\sqrt{x+5}$.
Step 4 · Substitute
With $u=x+5$, the integral $\int_4^{11}\sqrt{x+5}\,dx$ becomes…
$du=dx$, and the limits move to $u=9$ and $u=16$, as in the professor's side calculation on notes p. 3.
Step 5 · Evaluate

$\tfrac23u^{3/2}\Big|_9^{16}=\tfrac23(64-27)=\tfrac{74}{3}\approx24.667$ (verified by hand, SymPy and Wolfram).
Step 6 · Sanity check
The straight chord from $(4,g(4))$ to $(11,g(11))$ has length about $24.659$. Is $\frac{74}{3}\approx24.667$ believable?
Always compare with the chord: arc length ≥ chord, with equality only for a straight line.
Takeaway. Differentiate, square, add 1, simplify, then integrate. Here that meant a u-substitution with changed limits. Check the result against the straight chord.

Stage 5 · Compare

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

✗ Tempting
“The arc length of $y=\sin x$ on $[0,2\pi]$ is $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx=0$.” Or: “It's $2\pi$, the distance along the axis.”
✓ Correct
Length uses the slope: $\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}\,dx\approx7.64$, longer than the chord $2\pi\approx6.28$. It has no elementary antiderivative, so the notes estimate it: $T_4=\pi(\sqrt2+1)\approx7.58$ (3.3–3.5 notes p. 4).

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Integrating $f$ instead of $\sqrt{1+f'^2}$

Why it happens
Area problems trained you to integrate the function itself.

Prevent it
Length depends on steepness, so the derivative has to appear. Ask what's being added up: tiny pieces of curve, each about $\sqrt{1+f'^2}\,dx$ long.

Forgetting to square, or forgetting the 1

Why it happens
There are three operations in a row: differentiate, square, add 1.

Prevent it
Write $1+f'(x)^2$ on its own line and simplify it before you touch the root.

Splitting the square root

Why it happens
$\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt a\sqrt b$ is true, so $\sqrt{a+b}=\sqrt a+\sqrt b$ feels natural.

Prevent it
Test with numbers: $\sqrt{9+16}=5$, but $3+4=7$. Simplify inside the root instead.

Losing the chain-rule factor in the substitution

Why it happens
With $u=4x+9$ it's easy to forget that $du=4\,dx$.

Prevent it
Write $du=\frac{du}{dx}\,dx$ every time (the Key on notes p. 3), and change the limits.

A parametrization that goes around twice

Why it happens
The integral measures distance traveled, whatever path the particle takes.

Prevent it
Check that the curve is traced once. For $(\cos t,\sin t)$, $t$ from 0 to $2\pi$ is one full circle; up to $4\pi$ is two.

Expecting an exact answer every time

Why it happens
Textbook examples are chosen so that they simplify.

Prevent it
Most arc-length integrals, like $\int\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}\,dx$, have no elementary antiderivative. Notes p. 4 asks you to set up, then estimate.

Understanding. Why is the arc length of $y=f(x)$ over $[a,b]$ always at least $b-a$?
A particle moves along $(\cos3t,\ \sin3t)$ for $0\le t\le\pi$. (a) How far does it travel? (b) What is the length of the curve it traces (the set of points it passes through)?
The speed is $\sqrt{9\sin^23t+9\cos^23t}=3$.
The angle $3t$ runs from 0 to $3\pi$: one full turn plus half a turn. Which points does the particle visit?
(a) $\int_0^\pi3\,dt=3\pi\approx9.42$. (b) After one full turn the particle has visited every point of the unit circle; the last half turn repeats points it has already visited. So the curve traced is the whole unit circle, of length $2\pi\approx6.28$. The formula $\int\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}\,dt$ gives a curve's length only when it traces the curve once.
Find the arc length of $y=\dfrac{x^2}{2}-\dfrac{\ln x}{4}$ for $1\le x\le e$.
$y'=x-\frac{1}{4x}$. Expand $y'^2$, then add 1.
$1+y'^2=x^2+\frac12+\frac{1}{16x^2}$. Compare it with $\big(x+\frac1{4x}\big)^2$.
$1+y'^2=\big(x+\frac1{4x}\big)^2$, so the length is $\int_1^e\big(x+\frac1{4x}\big)dx=\big[\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{\ln x}{4}\big]_1^e=\frac{e^2}{2}+\frac14-\frac12=\frac{e^2}{2}-\frac14\approx3.4445$. It's the same trick as Practice 4: flip the sign of the cross term.

Explain why (Level 7)

In your own words: why does $\int_a^b\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\,dx$ give the length of the graph? Where do the 1, the $f'(x)$ and the square root come from?

A model explanation

Chop the curve into short pieces. Over a piece of width $\Delta x$ the curve rises about $\Delta y\approx f'(x)\,\Delta x$, so the piece is nearly the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $\Delta x$ and $f'(x)\,\Delta x$. By Pythagoras its length is $\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(f'(x)\Delta x)^2}=\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\,\Delta x$. The 1 is the horizontal leg per unit of $x$, $f'(x)$ is the vertical leg per unit of $x$, and the square root is Pythagoras. Adding the pieces is a Riemann sum, and as the pieces shrink it becomes the integral. Equivalently, a particle at $(t,f(t))$ has velocity $(1,f'(t))$, so the integral is its distance traveled (3.3–3.5 notes p. 2).
