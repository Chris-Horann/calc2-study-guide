Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

The center of mass is the balance point: “the point where we can place the fulcrum to balance” (3.7–3.9 notes p. 6). On a seesaw, two masses balance when $m_1d_1=m_2d_2$: a heavy mass close to the fulcrum balances a light one far away. Solving for the fulcrum gives $\bar x=\dfrac{m_1x_1+m_2x_2}{m_1+m_2}$, a weighted average of the positions, where each position counts as much as the mass sitting there.

A wire or a plate is a huge number of tiny masses. Each piece contributes its mass to the bottom of that average and position × mass (its moment) to the top. Sums become integrals: $\bar x=\dfrac{\int x\,dm}{\int dm}=\dfrac{\bar xM}{M}$. The bottom is the mass from §3.7; the top is the new part.

What to notice: on the left, the 60 kg child sits half as far from the fulcrum as the 30 kg child, so $30\cdot2=60\cdot1$ and the seesaw balances at $x=2$, not at the middle, $x=1.5$. On the right (notes p. 12), each vertical strip of the plate is replaced by one dot at the strip's midpoint, with a size that shows the strip's mass. The center of mass $(\frac32,\frac12)$ is the weighted average of those dots.
An everyday version

Balance a hammer on one finger. You don't hold it at the middle of the handle; you hold it near the heavy head. Carrying a long plank, you grip it at its balance point so neither end drops. Both are the center of mass: the place where the turning effect of everything on the left exactly cancels everything on the right.

Three questions to ask every time
  1. What is the total mass $M$? The §3.7 integral: $\int\delta\,dx$ or $\int\delta(f-g)\,dx$.
  2. What is the moment? Multiply each piece's mass by its position: $x$ for $\bar x$, and the strip's midpoint height $\frac{f+g}2$ for $\bar y$.
  3. Divide. $\bar x=\bar xM/M$ and $\bar y=\bar yM/M$. Then check: is it inside the object's range, and on the heavier side?

From the seesaw to an integral (3.7–3.9 notes pp. 6–7)

$$m_1(\bar x-x_1)=m_2(x_2-\bar x)\ \Rightarrow\ (m_1+m_2)\,\bar x=m_1x_1+m_2x_2\ \Rightarrow\ \bar x=\frac{\sum m_ix_i}{\sum m_i}$$ $$\text{wire: }\ \bar x\approx\frac{\sum x_i\,\delta(x_i)\Delta x}{\sum\delta(x_i)\Delta x}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{\int_a^b x\,\delta(x)\,dx}{\int_a^b\delta(x)\,dx}$$

Each piece of wire is a point mass $\delta(x_i)\Delta x$ at position $x_i$. So the wire is “$n$ masses on a line”, and the point-mass formula applies. The labels in green on the notes: the top is $\bar xM$, the $x$-component of the moment; the bottom is $M$, the total mass.

Why balance means “moment ÷ mass”

Put a knife edge under the object at $x=c$. A bit of mass $dm$ at position $x$ turns the object with strength $(x-c)\,dm$: positive on the right, negative on the left. It balances when the total turning effect is zero:

$$\int(x-c)\,dm=0\ \iff\ \int x\,dm=c\int dm\ \iff\ c=\frac{\int x\,dm}{\int dm}=\frac{\bar xM}{M}.$$

That's $m_1d_1=m_2d_2$ again, with every bit of mass included. It also explains the name: $\bar xM$ is literally $\bar x$ times $M$. The whole object behaves like a single mass $M$ sitting at $\bar x$.

Why $\bar y$ uses the strip's midpoint, $\frac{f(x)+g(x)}{2}$

For $\bar x$, every point of a vertical strip has the same $x$, so the strip's position is just $x$. For $\bar y$, the points of a strip have different heights, from $g(x)$ up to $f(x)$. But the density is constant along the strip (it depends only on $x$), so the strip balances at its middle, $y_i=\frac{f(x_i)+g(x_i)}2$ (notes p. 11). Replace each strip by a point mass at its midpoint and average again: $\bar y=\dfrac{\int\frac{f+g}2\,\delta\,(f-g)\,dx}{\int\delta\,(f-g)\,dx}$. The textbook writes the top as $\int\delta\,\frac{f^2-g^2}{2}\,dx$, which is the same thing because $(f+g)(f-g)=f^2-g^2$.

A notation trap

Your professor writes the moments as $\bar xM$ and $\bar yM$, so $\bar x=\bar xM/M$ reads off directly. The textbook calls the same quantities $M_x$ and $M_y$. Many other books (e.g. Stewart, OpenStax) use the opposite subscripts: their $M_y=\int x\,dm$ is “the moment about the $y$-axis”. If you look something up online, check which integral the symbol stands for. The professor's notation can't be swapped by accident.

The object and its density are symmetric about a lineThe center of mass is on that line; no integral needed for that coordinate.
“Half the mass on each side”That's the mass midpoint $x_m$ (§3.7), a different point.
Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“$\delta(x)$ is on the top and the bottom, so cancel it: $\bar x=\frac{\int x\,dx}{\int dx}$.” That's the middle of the wire, whatever the density, which can't be right: a wire with a heavy right end balances to the right. Integrals don't cancel like fractions (textbook Remark 3.8.4). Only a constant density can be pulled out of both and cancelled.

Guided example

A wire has density $\delta(x)=4x^2+2$ from $x=0$ to $x=3$. Find (1) the total mass $M$, (2) the $x$-component of the moment $\bar xM$, and (3) the $x$-coordinate of the center of mass $\bar x$. This is 3.7–3.9 notes p. 8.

Step 1 · Mass

$\big(\frac43x^3+2x\big)\Big|_0^3=36+6=42$.
Step 2 · The moment's integrand

position × mass of a piece: $x\cdot(4x^2+2)\,dx=(4x^3+2x)\,dx$.
Step 3 · Evaluate the moment

$\big(x^4+x^2\big)\Big|_0^3=81+9=90$.
Step 4 · Divide

$\frac{90}{42}=\frac{15}7\approx2.143$, as on notes p. 8.
Step 5 · Sanity check
The wire runs from 0 to 3, so its middle is 1.5. Does $\bar x\approx2.14$ make sense?
Two quick checks for any center of mass: it must lie within the object's range, and it leans toward the heavier side.
Step 6 · Not the mass midpoint
For this wire, $\int_0^{t}(4x^2+2)\,dx=21$ (half the mass) at $t\approx2.31$. Compared with $\bar x\approx2.14$:
Notes p. 9 asks for both, for exactly this reason. Mass on the light left side sits far from the right end, so it has a long lever arm. It pulls the balance point left more than it shifts the half-mass point.
Takeaway. Mass first ($\int\delta\,dx$), then the moment (the same integrand times $x$), then divide: $\bar x=\bar xM/M$. Check that it lies on the object, toward its heavy side.
A thin metal plate occupies the region between $f(x)=x$ and $g(x)=x^2$ for $0\le x\le1$, with area density $\delta(x)=2x+1$. Find its total mass $M$ and its center of mass $(\bar x,\bar y)$.
  1. Which curve is on top for $0<x<1$? What's a strip's height, and its midpoint?
  2. Write the strip's mass $\delta\,(f-g)\,dx$ and expand it.
  3. Find $M$, $\bar xM$ (times $x$) and $\bar yM$ (times the midpoint), then divide.
At $x=\frac12$: $x=0.5$, $x^2=0.25$. So $f=x$ is on top, the height is $x-x^2$, and the midpoint is $\frac{x+x^2}2$.
$\delta(f-g)=(2x+1)(x-x^2)=x+x^2-2x^3$. So $M=\int_0^1(x+x^2-2x^3)\,dx$.
$\bar yM=\int_0^1\frac{x+x^2}2(2x+1)(x-x^2)\,dx=\frac12\int_0^1(2x+1)(x^2-x^4)\,dx$.

$M=\frac12+\frac13-\frac12=\frac13$. $\bar xM=\int_0^1(x^2+x^3-2x^4)\,dx=\frac13+\frac14-\frac25=\frac{11}{60}$, so $\bar x=\frac{11}{60}\div\frac13=\frac{11}{20}$. $\bar yM=\frac12\int_0^1(2x^3+x^2-2x^5-x^4)\,dx=\frac12\big(\frac12+\frac13-\frac13-\frac15\big)=\frac3{20}$, so $\bar y=\frac{9}{20}$.

Check: with a constant density the centroid would be $(\frac12,\frac25)$. The density $2x+1$ is larger on the right, and the right-hand strips also sit higher, so both coordinates move up: $(0.55,0.45)$. Both are inside the region's box $[0,1]\times[0,1]$.

The clue to recognize next time: “center of mass of a plate” → strip mass $\delta(f-g)\,dx$, then times $x$ for $\bar x$ and times $\frac{f+g}2$ for $\bar y$, each divided by $M$.

Stage 5 · Compare

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

✗ Tempting
“$\delta$ is in the top and the bottom, so it cancels”: for $\delta=4x^2+2$ on $[0,3]$, $\bar x=\frac{\int_0^3x\,dx}{\int_0^3dx}=1.5$.
✓ Correct
$\bar x=\frac{\int_0^3x(4x^2+2)\,dx}{\int_0^3(4x^2+2)\,dx}=\frac{90}{42}=\frac{15}7\approx2.14$ (notes p. 8). Integrals don't cancel like fractions.
Clue for next time: if your center of mass ignores the density, it's the centroid. That's only correct when the density is constant (textbook Remark 3.8.4).

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Cancelling $\delta(x)$ between the two integrals

Why it happens
It looks like a fraction with the same factor on top and bottom.

Prevent it
Integrals add up products; you can't cancel inside them. Only a constant density factors out and cancels (textbook Remark 3.8.4).

Dividing by the length or area instead of the mass

Why it happens
“Average position” suggests dividing by the size of the object.

Prevent it
The weights in this average are masses, so you divide by the total mass $M$. Dividing by the area only works for a constant density (then it's the centroid).

Using $f$ (or $y$) instead of $\frac{f+g}2$ for $\bar y$

Why it happens
The top curve is the most visible thing in the sketch.

Prevent it
Ask: where does this strip balance? At its middle, $\frac{f+g}2$ (notes p. 11). Check the $\frac12$.

Leaving the strip height out of the moments

Why it happens
The extra factors $x$ and $\frac{f+g}2$ draw attention away from the rest.

Prevent it
Every moment is position × (the same strip mass as in $M$). Write $dm=\delta(f-g)\,dx$ once and reuse it.

Reporting $\bar xM$, or the mass midpoint, as the answer

Why it happens
Notes pp. 8–9 ask for $M$, $x_m$, $\bar xM$ and $\bar x$ separately, and they're easy to mix up.

Prevent it
$\bar x$ must be a position on the object. A huge number (like 90 or 383,440) is a moment, not a position.

A coefficient slip in integration by parts

Why it happens
$\int e^{3x}dx=\frac13e^{3x}$, and the $\frac13$ appears twice in $\int xe^{3x}dx$. Notes p. 9 itself writes $\frac23$ where $\frac29$ belongs.

Prevent it
Differentiate your antiderivative to check it, and check that $\bar x$ lands on the object.

A wire lies along the $x$-axis from $x=0$ to $x=4$ feet with density $\delta(x)=e^{-x}$ slugs/ft (the wire of Practice 3 in §3.7). Find its center of mass.
$M=1-e^{-4}$. For the moment, $\int xe^{-x}\,dx=-xe^{-x}-e^{-x}+C$ (by parts).
$\bar xM=\big(-xe^{-x}-e^{-x}\big)\Big|_0^4=1-5e^{-4}$, so $\bar x=\frac{1-5e^{-4}}{1-e^{-4}}\approx0.9254$ ft, about 37% larger than the mass midpoint $x_m\approx0.6750$. (The textbook says “almost 50% larger”, which overstates it: the ratio is $1.37$.)
A wire lies on $2\le x\le7$ with density $\delta(x)=3x^2+2x+4$. Find (a) the $x$-component of the moment $\bar xM$ and (b) the center of mass $\bar x$.
$M=\big[x^3+x^2+4x\big]_2^7=420-20=400$.
$\bar xM=\int_2^7(3x^3+2x^2+4x)\,dx=\Big[\frac34x^4+\frac23x^3+2x^2\Big]_2^7$.
$\bar xM=\frac{25529}{12}-\frac{304}{12}=\frac{25225}{12}\approx2102.08$, so $\bar x=\frac{25225/12}{400}=\frac{1009}{192}\approx5.2552$, right of the middle (4.5) because the density grows.
A thin plate occupies the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$, with area density $\delta(x)=2-x^2$. Find $M$, $\bar x$ and $\bar y$.
Top $f=x$, bottom $g=-x$, so the height is $2x$ and the midpoint is $0$.
$M=\int_0^1(2-x^2)(2x)\,dx$, $\ \bar xM=\int_0^1x(2-x^2)(2x)\,dx$.
$M=\int_0^1(4x-2x^3)\,dx=2-\frac12=\frac32$. $\bar xM=\int_0^1(4x^2-2x^4)\,dx=\frac43-\frac25=\frac{14}{15}$, so $\bar x=\frac{14}{15}\cdot\frac23=\frac{28}{45}\approx0.622$. Every strip's midpoint is at $y=\frac{x+(-x)}2=0$, so $\bar y=0$ without integrating: the plate and its density are symmetric about the $x$-axis.
A thin plate occupies the region bounded on the top and left by $y=x^2$, on the right by $x=2$, and below by $y=1$. Its density is $\delta(x)=x$ kg/m². Find its center of mass.
$y=x^2$ meets $y=1$ at $x=1$, so the plate runs from $x=1$ to $x=2$. Height $x^2-1$, midpoint $\frac{x^2+1}2$.
$dm=x(x^2-1)\,dx$. $M=\int_1^2(x^3-x)\,dx=\frac94$.
$\bar xM=\int_1^2(x^4-x^2)\,dx=\frac{58}{15}$, so $\bar x=\frac{58}{15}\cdot\frac49=\frac{232}{135}\approx1.7185$. $\bar yM=\frac12\int_1^2(x^5-x)\,dx=\frac92$, so $\bar y=\frac92\cdot\frac49=2$.
Find the centroid (the center of mass with constant density) of the region between $f(x)=\sqrt x$ and $g(x)=x$ for $0\le x\le1$.
With a constant density $\delta$, it cancels: $\bar x=\frac{\int x(f-g)\,dx}{\int(f-g)\,dx}$, and the same for $\bar y$. The bottom is the area.
Area $=\int_0^1(\sqrt x-x)\,dx=\frac16$. $\int_0^1\frac{f^2-g^2}2\,dx=\frac12\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx$.
$\int_0^1x(\sqrt x-x)\,dx=\frac25-\frac13=\frac1{15}$, so $\bar x=\frac{1/15}{1/6}=\frac25$. $\frac12\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx=\frac1{12}$, so $\bar y=\frac{1/12}{1/6}=\frac12$. Check that it's inside the region: at $x=0.4$ the region runs from $y=0.4$ up to $\sqrt{0.4}\approx0.63$, and $\bar y=0.5$ is in between.

Recognition. For which of these can you find $\bar x$ without integrating? Select all that apply.
Why

A constant density balances at the middle (5). Equal masses balance halfway between them (4). A density that's symmetric about 0 on a symmetric interval balances at 0. The densities $3x^2$ and $e^{-x}$ are lopsided, so you need the integrals: $\int x\cdot3x^2dx/\int3x^2dx=\frac32$, and $\frac{1-5e^{-4}}{1-e^{-4}}\approx0.925$ (Practice 5).

Understanding. Why does the $\bar y$ integral for a plate use $\frac{f(x)+g(x)}{2}$?
Masses $3$, $6$, $4$ and $9$ sit at the points $(-1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,0)$ and $(0,-1)$ in the plane. Find the center of mass $(\bar x,\bar y)$ of the system.
The seesaw formula works one coordinate at a time: $\bar x=\frac{\sum m_ix_i}{\sum m_i}$ and $\bar y=\frac{\sum m_iy_i}{\sum m_i}$.
$\sum m_i=22$. For $\bar x$ only the masses on the $x$-axis have $x\ne0$.
$\bar x=\frac{3(-1)+4(1)}{22}=\frac1{22}$ and $\bar y=\frac{6(1)+9(-1)}{22}=-\frac3{22}$. The point is a little right (4 beats 3) and below (9 beats 6) the origin.
Find the centroid (constant density) of the half-disk under $y=\sqrt{16-x^2}$, $-4\le x\le4$.
Symmetry gives $\bar x$. For $\bar y$ you need the area, which you know from geometry: half of $\pi\cdot4^2$.
$\bar y=\frac{1}{A}\int_{-4}^4\frac{f^2-0^2}{2}\,dx=\frac1{8\pi}\int_{-4}^4\frac{16-x^2}{2}\,dx$. No square root survives.
$\bar x=0$ by symmetry. $\int_{-4}^4\frac{16-x^2}2\,dx=\frac12\big(128-\frac{128}3\big)=\frac{128}3$, so $\bar y=\frac{128/3}{8\pi}=\frac{16}{3\pi}\approx1.698$, a bit below half the radius. Writing the midpoint factor as $\frac{f^2-g^2}{2}$ (textbook Prop. 3.8.6) is what makes the square root disappear.

Explain why (Level 7)

Why is the center of mass $\bar x=\bar xM/M$? And why isn't it the same point as the mass midpoint from §3.7?

A model explanation

An object balances on a knife edge at $c$ when the turning effects cancel: every bit of mass $dm$ at $x$ pushes with strength $(x-c)\,dm$, so balance means $\int(x-c)\,dm=0$. Rearranged, $c=\int x\,dm\big/\int dm$: the moment divided by the mass. For two masses that's $m_1d_1=m_2d_2$ (notes p. 6), and for a wire it's $\int x\delta\,dx\big/\int\delta\,dx$ (notes p. 7). It's a weighted average of position, with mass as the weight. The mass midpoint is a different question: where is half the mass on each side? It ignores how far the mass is from the cut. A small amount of mass far away counts a lot toward balance (long lever arm) but only a little toward half the mass. So the two points differ unless the density is constant or symmetric: on notes p. 9, $x_m\approx3.785$ while $\bar x\approx3.719$. It's like a median versus a mean.
