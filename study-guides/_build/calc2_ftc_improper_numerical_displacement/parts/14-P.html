Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

Substitution undoes the chain rule. Integration by parts undoes the product rule. It's the tool for a product of two different kinds of functions, like $x\,e^x$ or $x^4\ln x$, when there's no inside/derivative pair for substitution.

Parts doesn't finish an integral by itself. It trades the integral you have for a different one: $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$. The whole skill is choosing $u$ and $dv$ so the new integral, $\int v\,du$, is easier than the one you started with.

A picture of the trade

Think of the curve $(u,v)$ drawn in a $u$–$v$ plane. $\int u\,dv$ is the area to the left of the curve and $\int v\,du$ is the area under it. Together they fill a rectangle of area $uv$. So if one region is hard to find, compute the other and subtract from the rectangle. The explorer in Stage 3 draws this.

Three questions to ask every time
  1. Is it a product of two different kinds of functions, with no substitution available?
  2. Which factor gets simpler when I differentiate it? That's $u$ (LIATE, notes p. 8). Everything else, including $dx$, is $dv$.
  3. Is $\int v\,du$ easier than what I started with? If it's harder, swap the choice.

Why it works (notes p. 7)

  1. Product rule: $\big(f(x)g(x)\big)'=f(x)g'(x)+g(x)f'(x)$.
  2. Integrate both sides: $f(x)g(x)=\int f(x)g'(x)\,dx+\int g(x)f'(x)\,dx$.
  3. Rearrange: $\int f(x)g'(x)\,dx=f(x)g(x)-\int g(x)f'(x)\,dx$.
  4. Rename, as the professor does: $u=f$, $dv=g'\,dx$, $v=g$, $du=f'\,dx$.

LIATE: which factor is u? (notes p. 8)

Go down the list and let $u$ be the first type that appears:

L logarithmic ($\ln x$) → I inverse trig ($\tan^{-1}x$, $\sin^{-1}x$) → A algebraic ($x$, $x^2$, …) → T trig ($\sin$, $\cos$) → E exponential ($e^x$)

“Once $u$ is selected, everything else (including $dx$) is $dv$.” LIATE is a summary of the real reason: logs and inverse trig become algebraic when differentiated, and powers of $x$ shrink, while $e^x$, $\sin$ and $\cos$ never get simpler either way.

A polynomial of degree n needs n rounds (notes p. 10)

Each round differentiates the polynomial once. For $\int 2t^2e^{-t}\,dt$: round 1 gives $-2t^2e^{-t}+\int 4te^{-t}\,dt$, and round 2 handles $\int 4te^{-t}\,dt$. The result is $-2(t^2+2t+2)e^{-t}+C$. (Notes p. 10's last line has a copying slip; see the Compare stage.)

Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“For $\int xe^x\,dx$, let $u=e^x$, since $e^x$ is so easy to differentiate.” Then $dv=x\,dx$, $v=\frac{x^2}{2}$, and the new integral is $\int\frac{x^2}{2}e^x\,dx$: the power went up. $e^x$ is equally easy to integrate, so it belongs in $dv$. Let $u=x$, which becomes 1.

Stage 3 · Explore

Integration by parts as area

Two regions, one rectangle: see why ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du

presets · sliders a and b · hover the curve
As $x$ runs from $a$ to $b$, the point $(u(x),v(x))$ traces a curve. Blue (vertical strips $v\,du$) is $\int v\,du$; orange (horizontal strips $u\,dv$) is $\int u\,dv$.
$$u=x,\ dv=e^x\,dx\ \Rightarrow\ du=dx,\ v=e^x:\qquad \int xe^x\,dx=xe^x-\int e^x\,dx=xe^x-e^x+C$$
$$u=\ln x,\ dv=x^4\,dx\ \Rightarrow\ du=\tfrac{dx}{x},\ v=\tfrac{x^5}{5}:\qquad \int x^4\ln x\,dx=\tfrac{x^5}{5}\ln x-\tfrac{x^5}{25}+C$$
$$u=\tan^{-1}p,\ dv=2p\,dp\ \Rightarrow\ du=\tfrac{dp}{1+p^2},\ v=p^2:\qquad \int 2p\tan^{-1}p\,dp=p^2\tan^{-1}p-p+\tan^{-1}p+C$$
$$u=\sin^{-1}x,\ dv=dx\ \Rightarrow\ du=\tfrac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}},\ v=x:\qquad \int\sin^{-1}x\,dx=x\sin^{-1}x+\sqrt{1-x^2}+C$$
What to notice
  1. With Notes p. 8: ∫x eˣ dx on $[0,1]$: blue $=\int_0^1e^x\,dx=e-1\approx1.718$ and orange $=\int_0^1xe^x\,dx=1$. Together they're $e\approx2.718=u(1)v(1)-u(0)v(0)$, the whole dashed rectangle.
  2. Slide $a$ above 0. A corner rectangle $u(a)v(a)$ is cut out, and the two regions still add to $u(b)v(b)-u(a)v(a)$. That difference is the $[uv]_a^b$ in the formula.
  3. Orange is the integral you want and blue is the one parts gives you instead. The choice $u=x$ is good because blue is just $\int e^x\,dx$.
  4. Pick Notes p. 11: ∫sin⁻¹x dx. Now $v=x$, so orange is $\int\sin^{-1}x\,dx$ itself, and blue is $\int\frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$, which a substitution finishes.
Choose $u$ for $\displaystyle\int x\,e^x\,dx$ (notes p. 8). What does the new integral $\int v\,du$ look like?
$du=dx$, $v=e^x$: $\int xe^x\,dx=xe^x-\int e^x\,dx=xe^x-e^x+C$. The new integral is a basic rule.
$du=e^x\,dx$, $v=\frac{x^2}{2}$: $\frac{x^2}{2}e^x-\int\frac{x^2}{2}e^x\,dx$. The new integral has a higher power of $x$. Worse.
$du=(x+1)e^x\,dx$, $v=x$: $x^2e^x-\int(x^2+x)e^x\,dx$. Also worse: the leftover integral contains the original and more.
Choose $u$ for $\displaystyle\int x^4\ln x\,dx$ (notes p. 9).
$du=\frac{dx}{x}$, $v=\frac{x^5}{5}$: $\frac{x^5}{5}\ln x-\int\frac{x^5}{5}\cdot\frac1x\,dx=\frac{x^5}{5}\ln x-\int\frac{x^4}{5}\,dx=\frac{x^5}{5}\ln x-\frac{x^5}{25}+C$. Differentiating $\ln x$ turned it algebraic, and the $\frac1x$ cancels a power.
You'd need $v=\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-x$, which is itself a parts problem. Then the new integral $\int(x\ln x-x)\,4x^3\,dx$ still contains $\ln x$. You're going in circles. This is why L comes first in LIATE.
$\displaystyle\int\sin^{-1}x\,dx$ has no product in it (notes p. 11). What now?
The “multiply by 1” trick: $du=\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$, $v=x$. Then $x\sin^{-1}x-\int\frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$, and $w=1-x^2$ finishes it: $x\sin^{-1}x+\sqrt{1-x^2}+C$. The same trick gives $\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-x+C$ (textbook Example 1.1.21).
Every integrand is a product: $\sin^{-1}x\cdot1$. Choosing $dv=dx$ is always allowed, and it's exactly what you want when $u$ has a nice derivative but no known antiderivative.
Stage 4 · Attempt

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

Find $\displaystyle\int x\,e^{3x}\,dx$ (textbook §1.1 Suggested Ex. 13). Answer each question before the explanation appears.

Step 1 · Choose u
Which choice follows the Key (u gets simpler when differentiated)?
A (algebraic) comes before E (exponential) in LIATE: $u=x$ becomes $du=dx$. Everything else, $e^{3x}\,dx$, is $dv$.
Step 2 · Find v

$v=\tfrac13e^{3x}$ (a quick substitution $w=3x$). For $v$, any antiderivative works, so take $C=0$.
Step 3 · The new integral

Now $uv-\int v\,du=\tfrac x3e^{3x}-\int\tfrac13e^{3x}\,dx$.

$\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac13e^{3x}=\tfrac19e^{3x}$. The new integral was a basic rule, which is what a good choice of $u$ looks like.
Step 4 · Put it together

$\tfrac x3e^{3x}-\tfrac19e^{3x}+C$.
Step 5 · Check
Differentiate $\tfrac x3e^{3x}-\tfrac19e^{3x}$. Which work is right?
The product rule rebuilds $xe^{3x}$ plus an extra $\tfrac13e^{3x}$, and the $-\tfrac19e^{3x}$ term exists precisely to cancel it. Parts and the product rule are two views of the same fact.
Takeaway. Box first ($u$, $dv$ / $du$, $v$), with $u$ the factor that simplifies. Then $uv-\int v\,du$. The new integral should be a step closer to a basic rule. Differentiate to check.
Find $\displaystyle\int t\sin(2t)\,dt.$
  1. What kinds of functions are multiplied together? Where do they sit in LIATE?
  2. Which factor gets simpler when you differentiate it?
  3. What is $\int\sin(2t)\,dt$? Watch the inside $2t$.
Algebraic ($t$) before trig ($\sin 2t$): $u=t$, $dv=\sin(2t)\,dt$.
$du=dt$ and $v=-\tfrac12\cos(2t)$. Plug into $uv-\int v\,du$.
$-\tfrac t2\cos 2t-\int\big(-\tfrac12\cos 2t\big)\,dt=-\tfrac t2\cos 2t+\tfrac12\int\cos 2t\,dt$.
  1. $u=t$, $dv=\sin 2t\,dt$; $du=dt$, $v=-\tfrac12\cos 2t$.
  2. $\int t\sin 2t\,dt=-\tfrac t2\cos 2t+\tfrac12\int\cos 2t\,dt=-\tfrac t2\cos 2t+\tfrac14\sin 2t+C.$

Check: $\frac{d}{dt}\big(-\tfrac t2\cos 2t+\tfrac14\sin 2t\big)=-\tfrac12\cos 2t+t\sin 2t+\tfrac12\cos 2t=t\sin 2t$ ✓. Notes p. 9 does the same thing for $(x+6)\sin 2x$.

Stage 5 · Compare

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

✗ Tempting
“$\int x\cos x\,dx=\int x\,dx\cdot\int\cos x\,dx=\frac{x^2}{2}\sin x+C$.”
✓ Correct
The integral of a product is not the product of the integrals (differentiate $\frac{x^2}{2}\sin x$ to see). Parts: $u=x$, $v=\sin x$, so $x\sin x-\int\sin x\,dx=x\sin x+\cos x+C$.
Clue for next time: there's no product rule for integrals except parts itself, and parts always leaves a “$-\int v\,du$”.

Spot the error. A student computes $\displaystyle\int x\cos x\,dx$.

What went wrong

The formula is $uv\,\mathbf{-}\int v\,du$. Correct: $x\sin x-\int\sin x\,dx=x\sin x+\cos x+C$. Differentiating the student's answer gives $\sin x+x\cos x+\sin x\ne x\cos x$.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Putting the wrong factor in $u$

Why it happens
$e^x$ and $\sin x$ look like the “easy” factor to differentiate. But easy to differentiate isn't the goal; getting simpler is.

Prevent it
Use LIATE, then glance at $\int v\,du$ before computing it. If it looks harder than the original, swap.

Losing the minus sign in $uv-\int v\,du$

Why it happens
There are often two minus signs in play: the one in the formula and one inside $v$ (such as $v=-\cos x$).

Prevent it
Write $uv-\int v\,du$ with parentheses around $v$: $x(\sin x)-\int(\sin x)\,dx$. Then differentiate your answer.

Dropping the constant when finding $v$

Why it happens
$v$ is found in a side calculation, and the inside function's factor ($\tfrac12$ in $\int\sin 2t\,dt$) is easy to miss.

Prevent it
Find $v$ as carefully as any other integral. Notes p. 9 does it with a side substitution $w=2x$.

Stopping after one round

Why it happens
The first round leaves a new integral that looks almost done.

Prevent it
A polynomial of degree $n$ needs $n$ rounds (notes p. 10: $t^2$ needs two). Keep going until no integral sign is left.

Using parts when substitution was there all along

Why it happens
Any product looks like a parts problem.

Prevent it
First check for an inside function whose derivative is present: $x\,e^{x^2}$ is substitution, and $\frac{\ln x}{x}$ is substitution with $u=\ln x$ (textbook Additional Ex. 15b).

Stage 6 · Practice

Practice: standard applications

Find $\displaystyle\int x^4\ln x\,dx.$
Log before algebraic: $u=\ln x$, $dv=x^4\,dx$.
$du=\frac{dx}{x}$, $v=\frac{x^5}{5}$. The new integral is $\int\frac{x^5}{5}\cdot\frac1x\,dx=\int\frac{x^4}{5}\,dx$.
$\tfrac15x^5\ln x-\tfrac{1}{25}x^5+C$ (notes p. 9).
Find $\displaystyle\int (x+6)\sin(2x)\,dx.$
$u=x+6$ (algebraic), $dv=\sin 2x\,dx$.
$du=dx$, $v=-\tfrac12\cos 2x$.
$-\tfrac12(x+6)\cos 2x+\tfrac12\int\cos 2x\,dx=-\tfrac12(x+6)\cos 2x+\tfrac14\sin 2x+C$ (notes p. 9).
Find $\displaystyle\int 2t^2e^{-t}\,dt.$ Two rounds of parts.
Round 1: $u=2t^2$, $dv=e^{-t}\,dt$, so $du=4t\,dt$, $v=-e^{-t}$.
You get $-2t^2e^{-t}+4\int te^{-t}\,dt$. Round 2: $u=t$, $dv=e^{-t}\,dt$.
$\int te^{-t}\,dt=-te^{-t}-e^{-t}$, so the answer is $-2t^2e^{-t}-4te^{-t}-4e^{-t}+C=-2(t^2+2t+2)e^{-t}+C$.
⚠ Discrepancy in the notes

Notes p. 10 writes the last term as $-4t^{-t}$. It should be $-4e^{-t}$, as the notes' own side work shows. Verified by differentiating, SymPy and Wolfram.

Find $\displaystyle\int \sin^{-1}x\,dx.$
Multiply by 1: $u=\sin^{-1}x$, $dv=dx$.
$du=\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$, $v=x$. For $\int\frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$ use $w=1-x^2$.
$x\sin^{-1}x-\int\frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$. With $w=1-x^2$, $x\,dx=-\tfrac12dw$: $\int\frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}=-\sqrt{1-x^2}$. So the answer is $x\sin^{-1}x+\sqrt{1-x^2}+C$ (notes p. 11).
Find $\displaystyle\int e^{\sqrt x}\,dx.$ There's no product… yet.
Substitute first: $t=\sqrt x$, so $x=t^2$ and $dx=2t\,dt$.
The integral becomes $2\int te^t\,dt$: now it's a parts problem with $u=t$.
$2\big(te^t-e^t\big)+C=2\sqrt x\,e^{\sqrt x}-2e^{\sqrt x}+C$ (notes p. 12). Substitution exposed the product.
Find $\displaystyle\int (x-5)^2e^x\,dx.$
Degree 2, so two rounds. Round 1: $u=(x-5)^2$, $dv=e^x\,dx$.
You get $(x-5)^2e^x-\int 2(x-5)e^x\,dx$. Round 2: $u=2(x-5)$.
$(x-5)^2e^x-\big[2(x-5)e^x-2e^x\big]+C=e^x\big[(x-5)^2-2(x-5)+2\big]+C=e^x(x^2-12x+37)+C$.
Find $\displaystyle\int\tan^{-1}w\,dw.$
Multiply by 1, as on notes p. 11: $u=\tan^{-1}w$, $dv=dw$.
$w\tan^{-1}w-\int\frac{w\,dw}{1+w^2}$. The leftover is a substitution: $z=1+w^2$.
$\int\frac{w\,dw}{1+w^2}=\tfrac12\ln(1+w^2)$, so the answer is $w\tan^{-1}w-\tfrac12\ln(1+w^2)+C$.
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{\ln t}{t^2}\,dt.$
Log first: $u=\ln t$, $dv=t^{-2}\,dt$.
$du=\frac{dt}{t}$, $v=-\frac1t$. The new integral is $\int\frac{1}{t^2}\,dt$.
$-\frac{\ln t}{t}-\int\big(-\tfrac1t\big)\tfrac1t\,dt=-\frac{\ln t}{t}+\int t^{-2}\,dt=-\frac{\ln t}{t}-\frac1t+C$.
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^e\ln x\,dx.$
First find $\int\ln x\,dx$ by the multiply-by-1 trick.
$\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-x$. Evaluate from 1 to $e$.
$\big[x\ln x-x\big]_1^e=(e-e)-(0-1)=1$.

Why

Understanding. For $\int x^4\ln x\,dx$, why is $u=\ln x$ better than $u=x^4$?
Find $\displaystyle\int\ln(x^2+1)\,dx.$ (You may want the notes p. 10 trick: $x^2=(1+x^2)-1$.)
Multiply by 1: $u=\ln(x^2+1)$, $dv=dx$. Then $du=\frac{2x}{x^2+1}\,dx$ and $v=x$.
The new integral is $\int\frac{2x^2}{x^2+1}\,dx$. Rewrite $\frac{x^2}{x^2+1}=1-\frac{1}{1+x^2}$.
$x\ln(x^2+1)-2\int\Big(1-\frac{1}{1+x^2}\Big)dx=x\ln(x^2+1)-2x+2\tan^{-1}x+C$.

Two rounds: $\int x^2e^x\,dx=x^2e^x-2\int xe^x\,dx$.
$\int xe^x\,dx=xe^x-e^x$, so the antiderivative is $e^x(x^2-2x+2)$.

Explain why (Level 7)

In your own words: where does $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$ come from, and how do you decide which factor is $u$?

A model explanation

The product rule says $(uv)'=uv'+vu'$. Integrating both sides gives $uv=\int u\,dv+\int v\,du$, and moving one integral across gives the formula. So parts is the product rule read backwards. It trades $\int u\,dv$ for $\int v\,du$, and the trade only helps if the new integral is easier. That happens when $u$ gets simpler when differentiated (logs and inverse trig become algebraic, and powers shrink) while $dv$ can still be integrated. LIATE summarizes this: pick $u$ from the first type on the list, and put $e^x$, $\sin$, $\cos$ (which never simplify) in $dv$.
