Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

Work = force × distance, $W=Fd$, “when the quantities are constants” (3.7–3.9 notes p. 14). Lift a 100 lb rope 50 ft and you do $5000$ ft-lb of work. Calculus is needed when something isn't constant, and in your notes that happens in two different ways:

  • The force changes as the object moves. A spring pulls harder the more you stretch it; a leaking bucket gets lighter on the way up. Cut the path into short steps, use $F\cdot\Delta x$ on each, and add: $W=\int_a^bF(x)\,dx$ (notes p. 20).
  • Different pieces of the object move different distances. Pull a hanging rope onto a roof and the bottom of the rope travels 50 ft while the top barely moves; pump out a tank and the water at the bottom rises farthest. Cut the object into slices, use (weight of slice) × (distance it rises) on each, and add (notes pp. 16–19).
What to notice: it's the same 100 lb rope in both pictures. When a helicopter lifts it, every piece rises the full 50 ft, so $W=Fd=5000$ ft-lb with no integral (notes p. 15). When it's pulled onto a roof, the piece at height $y$ only rises $50-y$ ft, so you slice: $W=\int_0^{50}2(50-y)\,dy=2500$ ft-lb (notes p. 16), exactly half.
An everyday version

Carry a box of books up two flights of stairs and you've done twice the work of one flight. Now empty a full bathtub with a bucket over its rim: the first buckets from the top barely need lifting, the last ones from the bottom need the full height. Each bucketful is a “slice”: its weight times how far it goes.

Three questions to ask every time
  1. What isn't constant? The force along the path (∫F dx), or the distance for different pieces (slice the object)?
  2. For one piece: what force, and what distance? A slice's weight is density × volume, × 9.8 if the density is in kg/m³. Its distance is (where it goes) − (where it is).
  3. What are the limits, and the units? Integrate over where the stuff actually is (the water's depth, not the tank's height). Answers come in ft-lb or J.
Stage 2 · Understand

What it's built from, and why it works

Prerequisite map

  • Work
    • $W=Fd$ and its units
      Quick check: lift 20 lb through 3 ft.

      Quick check: what does 5 kg weigh?
      show$5\cdot9.8=49$ N. A density in lb/ft³ is already a weight: no 9.8.
    • A slab's volume (§3.5): area × thickness

    • Similar triangles

    • Hooke's law: $x$ is the stretch beyond natural length
      Quick check: natural length 10 in, stretched to 14 in. $x=$?
      show$4$ in, which is $\frac13$ ft (notes p. 21).

Two ways to slice (3.7–3.9 notes pp. 16, 20)

$$\text{along the path: }\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}F(x_i)\,\Delta x\ \longrightarrow\ \int_a^bF(x)\,dx$$ $$\text{across the object: }\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{(\text{weight of slice }i)}_{\text{e.g. }2\,\Delta y}\cdot\underbrace{(\text{distance it rises})}_{\text{e.g. }50-y_i}\ \longrightarrow\ \int_0^{50}2(50-y)\,dy$$

On a short step the force is nearly constant (the professor's green “$F\cdot d$” under $f(x_i)\Delta x$ on p. 20). On a thin slice, every bit of it rises the same distance. Either way $W=Fd$ is true for each piece, and the integral adds the pieces.

Why the slice's weight is “density × 9.8 × volume” in metric, but not in feet and pounds

The professor's steps on p. 17: $F_{\text{slice}}=M_{\text{slice}}\times9.8=\delta V_{\text{slice}}\times9.8=1000\cdot\Delta y\cdot5^2\cdot9.8$. Water's density 1000 kg/m³ is a mass per volume, so to get a force (newtons) you multiply by $g=9.8$ m/s².

On p. 19 the liquid “weighs 75 lb/ft³”. That's a weight per volume already (pounds are a force), so $F_{\text{slice}}=75\cdot\pi r^2\Delta y$ with no 9.8 and no 32. The units decide it: kg needs × 9.8 to become N; lb is already a force.

A check you already know from §3.8

For slicing problems, the total work equals (total weight) × (how far the center of mass rises) (textbook after Example 3.9.15). The rope on p. 16 weighs 100 lb and its center of mass rises from 25 ft to 50 ft: $100\times25=2500$ ✓. The water on p. 17 weighs $1000\cdot9.8\cdot225=2{,}205{,}000$ N, and its center of mass rises from 4.5 m to the spout at 14 m: $2{,}205{,}000\times9.5=20{,}947{,}500$ J ✓. It's a great check, but the integral is the method: for a cone you'd need the center of mass first, which is an integral anyway.

Recognition: what kind of work problem is it?

You notice…So you…
A constant force moves an object a fixed distance, or the whole object moves the same distance$W=Fd$, no integral (notes p. 15).
A spring, “natural length”, “equilibrium”Hooke's law $F=kx$. Find $k$ from the given data, then $W=\int kx\,dx$ (notes pp. 21–22).
A force that changes along the way, given as a function$W=\int_a^bF(x)\,dx$ (notes p. 20).
A hanging rope or chain pulled upSlice the rope: weight per foot × $\Delta y$ × distance to the top (notes p. 16).
“Pump the water out of the tank”Slice the liquid horizontally: weight of a slice × distance to the top or spout, integrated over the liquid's depth (notes pp. 17–19).
A leaking bucket lifted at a constant speedWrite the weight as a function of time, change to height ($y=vt$), then $\int F\,dy$ (notes p. 23).
The position (or speed) at two times is given, but no forceTotal work = change in kinetic energy, $\frac12mv_1^2-\frac12mv_0^2$ (notes p. 24).
Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“Total weight × total height.” For the rope pulled onto the roof that's $100\times50=5000$ ft-lb, twice the real answer. It pretends every piece travels the full 50 ft, when most of the rope starts partway up. It's only right when the whole object moves together (the helicopter, p. 15).

A rope weighs 2 lb/ft and is 50 ft long. Which job takes 2500 ft-lb of work?
Every piece rises the full 50 ft, so $W=Fd=100\times50=5000$ ft-lb.
The piece at height $y$ rises only $50-y$: $\int_0^{50}2(50-y)\,dy=2500$ ft-lb. On average a piece rises 25 ft.
The weight is the same, but the distances aren't. In the roof job the top of the rope hardly moves at all.
A tank holds a liquid weighing 75 lb/ft³ (notes p. 19). What is the weight of a slice of volume $\Delta V$ ft³?
“Weighing 75 lb/ft³” is a weight per volume, and pounds are a force. No conversion needed.
9.8 m/s² turns a mass in kg into newtons. It's for kg/m³ (notes pp. 17–18), not lb/ft³.
32 ft/s² would turn slugs (mass) into pounds. The 75 is already in pounds.
A tank 12 m tall is $\frac34$ full (9 m of water), and the water is pumped out of a spout 2 m above the top (notes p. 17). Which integral has the right limits and distance?
The water occupies $0\le y\le9$, and a slice at $y$ rises to the spout at $12+2=14$: $d_{\text{slice}}=12-y+2=14-y$ (notes p. 17).
There's no water above 9 m, so nothing there to lift. That overcounts: $23{,}520{,}000$ J instead of $20{,}947{,}500$ J.
That pumps to the top of the tank and forgets the spout's extra 2 m.
$y$ is how high the slice already is. The work depends on how far it still has to go.

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

A tank is an inverted right circular cone (vertex pointing down) with radius 5 m and height 12 m. It's full of water (density 1000 kg/m³). Find the work required to pump all of the water out of the top of the tank. This is 3.7–3.9 notes p. 18; the notes ask for the integral, and here you'll evaluate it too.

Step 1 · Slice
How should you slice the water?
“Subdivide water body horizontally” (notes p. 17). A slice at height $y$ with thickness $\Delta y$ is a thin disk.
Step 2 · The slice's radius

$r=\frac5{12}y$: zero at the vertex, 5 at the top.
Step 3 · The slice's weight

$F_{\text{slice}}=1000\cdot\pi\big(\frac5{12}y\big)^2\Delta y\cdot9.8$ newtons, as on notes p. 18.
Step 4 · The distance

$d_{\text{slice}}=12-y$.
Step 5 · Integrate

Pull out the constants: $9800\pi\cdot\frac{25}{144}\int_0^{12}(12y^2-y^3)\,dy=9800\pi\cdot\frac{25}{144}\big(6912-5184\big)=9800\pi\cdot\frac{25}{144}\cdot1728=2{,}940{,}000\pi\approx9.24\times10^6$ J.
Step 6 · Sanity check
The water weighs $9800\cdot\frac13\pi(5^2)(12)=980{,}000\pi$ N. Its center of mass is $\frac34$ of the way up from the vertex (textbook Example 3.8.9), at $y=9$. Does $2{,}940{,}000\pi$ make sense?
Most of the water in a vertex-down cone sits near the wide top, so it barely needs lifting: the work is only a quarter of “weight × 12”.
Takeaway. Slice horizontally. For the slice at height $y$: radius from similar triangles, weight = density × 9.8 × $\pi r^2\Delta y$, distance = (destination) − $y$. Integrate over the heights where there's water. Units: J.
A rectangular tank has length 20 ft, width 8 ft and height 30 ft. It contains water up to a height of 10 ft (the rest is empty). Find the work required to pump all of the water to the top of the tank. Water weighs 62.4 lb/ft³.
  1. Which way do you slice, and what is the volume of a slice at height $y$?
  2. What does that slice weigh? (Is 62.4 a mass or a weight?)
  3. How far does it rise, and over which heights do you integrate?
Every horizontal slice is a $20\times8$ rectangle, so its volume is $160\,\Delta y$ ft³.
Weight $62.4\cdot160\,\Delta y=9984\,\Delta y$ lb. Distance $30-y$. Water from $y=0$ to $y=10$.
$W=9984\int_0^{10}(30-y)\,dy$.

$W=9984\Big[30y-\frac{y^2}2\Big]_0^{10}=9984\cdot250=2{,}496{,}000$ ft-lb.

Check with §3.8: the water weighs $9984\cdot10=99{,}840$ lb, and its center of mass rises from 5 ft to 30 ft: $99{,}840\times25=2{,}496{,}000$ ✓.

Compare your approach

The clue to recognize next time: “pump the water out” → horizontal slices → (weight of slice) × (destination − y), integrated over the water's depth.

Stage 5 · Compare

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

✗ Tempting
“The rope weighs 100 lb and it ends up 50 ft higher, so $W=Fd=100\times50=5000$ ft-lb” for the rope pulled onto the roof.
✓ Correct
The piece at height $y$ rises $50-y$, so $W=\int_0^{50}2(50-y)\,dy=2500$ ft-lb (notes p. 16). $W=Fd$ needs every piece to move the same distance (notes p. 15).
Clue for next time: before writing $W=Fd$, ask whether every part of the object moves the same distance under the same force. If not, slice.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Using $W=Fd$ when pieces move different distances

Why it happens
$W=Fd$ is the formula you know, and the total weight and height are right there in the problem.

Prevent it
Ask whether the whole object moves together (p. 15) or piece by piece (p. 16). If piece by piece, slice.

Integrating over the tank's height instead of the liquid's depth

Why it happens
The tank's height is the biggest number in the problem.

Prevent it
The integral adds up the slices that exist. On p. 17 the water is 9 m deep in a 12 m tank: $\int_0^9$. The textbook calls this “a common mistake” (Example 3.9.15).

The wrong distance: $y$, or forgetting the spout

Why it happens
$y$ is the variable you just wrote down.

Prevent it
Distance = destination − where the slice is. On p. 17: $12-y+2=14-y$.

Multiplying by 9.8 (or 32) when the density is already a weight

Why it happens
Metric examples (pp. 17–18) always have the 9.8.

Prevent it
Look at the units. kg/m³ is a mass density: × 9.8 gives N/m³. lb/ft³ is a weight density: use it as is (p. 19).

A cone's radius from the wrong similar triangle

Why it happens
Vertex-up and vertex-down cones look alike on paper.

Prevent it
Check the radius at the bottom and at the top: vertex down gives 0 at $y=0$ (p. 18); vertex up gives the full radius at $y=0$ (p. 19).

Not converting inches to feet before finding $k$

Why it happens
The problem gives inches, and the arithmetic works either way.

Prevent it
Convert first (p. 21: 4 in $=\frac13$ ft). Otherwise your answer is in inch-pounds.

Integrating the leaky bucket's force with respect to time

Why it happens
The leak is given per second, so $F=32-0.2t$ comes out in $t$.

Prevent it
Work is force × distance: $W=\int F\,dy$. Change variables with $y=2t$ first: $F=32-0.1y$ (p. 23). Integrating $\int_0^{50}F\,dt$ gives 1350, which is in lb·s, not ft-lb.

Leaving off the units

Why it happens
The integral gives a bare number.

Prevent it
Every answer in the notes' §3.9 ends with its unit: (ft-lb), (J), (N/m). Pounds × feet = ft-lb; newtons × metres = J.

Stage 6 · Practice

Practice: standard applications

Professor's examples to redo, textbook exercises and new variants. You may type the unit after the number (e.g. 2500 ft-lb, 20 J, 30 N/m); commas are fine.

A rope hangs from a tall building. The rope weighs 2 lb/ft and is 50 ft long. How much work is required to pull the entire rope to the top of the building?
Put $y=0$ at the bottom of the rope. A piece of length $\Delta y$ at height $y$ weighs $2\Delta y$ lb.
It rises $50-y$ ft. $W=\int_0^{50}2(50-y)\,dy$.
$W=\big(100y-y^2\big)\Big|_0^{50}=5000-2500=2500$ ft-lb, as on notes p. 16. Check: 100 lb × the 25 ft its center of mass rises.
A rectangular tank with a square base of side length 5 m and height 12 m is $\frac34$ full of water. Find an integral that calculates the work required to pump all of the water out of a spout 2 m above the top of the tank, then evaluate it. Density of water is 1000 kg/m³.
Each slice is a $5\times5$ square of thickness $\Delta y$: $F_{\text{slice}}=1000\cdot25\,\Delta y\cdot9.8$ N.
$W=\int_0^9 25000\cdot9.8\cdot(14-y)\,dy=245{,}000\Big[14y-\frac{y^2}2\Big]_0^9$.
$W=\int_0^9 1000\cdot5^2\cdot9.8\cdot(14-y)\,dy$ (notes p. 17) $=245{,}000\,(126-40.5)=245{,}000\cdot85.5=20{,}947{,}500$ J $\approx2.09\times10^7$ J.
A tank in the shape of a right circular cone, vertex pointing up, has height 100 ft and base radius 20 ft. It is filled to a height of 80 ft with a liquid weighing 75 lb/ft³. Set up an integral for the work required to pump all the liquid to the top, then evaluate it.
$\frac{100-y}{100}=\frac{r}{20}$: the distance from the vertex, $100-y$, is to the full height as $r$ is to the full radius.
$W=\int_0^{80}75\pi\big(\frac{100-y}5\big)^2(100-y)\,dy=3\pi\int_0^{80}(100-y)^3\,dy$.
With $u=100-y$: $3\pi\int_{20}^{100}u^3\,du=\frac{3\pi}4\big(100^4-20^4\big)=74{,}880{,}000\pi\approx2.35\times10^8$ ft-lb. The notes stop at the integral; this is its value (SymPy, mpmath and Wolfram agree).
A force of 10 lb is required to hold a spring stretched 4 inches beyond its natural length. Find the spring constant (in lb/ft) and the work required to stretch it 6 inches beyond its natural length.
4 in $=\frac13$ ft and 6 in $=\frac12$ ft. $F(\frac13)=\frac13k=10$.
$W=\int_0^{1/2}30x\,dx$.
$k=30$ lb/ft. $W=15x^2\Big|_0^{1/2}=\frac{15}4$ ft-lb, as on notes p. 21.
Calculate the work required to compress a spring 2 meters from equilibrium when the spring constant is $k=10$ N/m.
Compression means $x$ goes from $0$ to $-2$. $F(x)=10x$.
$W=\int_0^{-2}10x\,dx=5x^2\Big|_0^{-2}=20$ J (notes p. 21). Stretching 2 m would also take 20 J.
The total work required to stretch a spring from its equilibrium position to 4 meters past equilibrium is 240 J. Find the spring constant $k$.
$W=\int_0^4kx\,dx=\frac12kx^2\Big|_0^4$.
$8k=240$, so $k=30$ N/m (notes p. 22).
A leaky bucket weighs 2 lb and is filled with 30 lb of water, to be lifted 100 ft. The bucket loses water at a constant rate of 0.2 lb/s and travels at 2 ft/s. How much work is done in lifting the bucket 100 feet? Ignore the weight of the rope.
At $t=0$ the force is 32 lb, and it drops 0.2 lb every second: $F=32-0.2t$.
The bucket rises 2 ft/s, so $y=2t$ and $t=\frac y2$. Write $F$ in terms of $y$.
$F=32-0.1y$, so $W=\int_0^{100}(32-0.1y)\,dy=\big(32y-0.05y^2\big)\Big|_0^{100}=3200-500=2700$ ft-lb (notes p. 23). The bucket reaches the top after 50 s, having lost 10 lb, so the water never runs out.
$x(t)=4-t^2$ is the position function, in meters, of a 10 kg object moving along the $x$-axis between times $t=0$ and $t=3$ seconds. Calculate the work done in displacing the object from $x(0)=4$ m to $x(3)=-5$ m.
“Total work = change in kinetic energy” (notes p. 24): $W=\frac12mv(3)^2-\frac12mv(0)^2$.
$v(t)=x'(t)=-2t$ m/s (§3.1: velocity is the derivative of position).
$v(0)=0$ and $v(3)=-6$, so $W=\frac12\cdot10\cdot36-0=180$ J. The direction of motion doesn't matter: kinetic energy uses $v^2$.
You lift a bucket of sand 60 feet at a constant velocity of 4 ft/s. It starts with 80 lb of sand, which leaks out at 0.5 lb per second. How much work is done lifting the sand? (Ignore the bucket and the rope.)
$F=80-0.5t$ and $y=4t$.
$F=80-0.5\cdot\frac y4=80-\frac y8$, so $W=\int_0^{60}\big(80-\frac y8\big)\,dy=4800-\frac{3600}{16}=4575$ ft-lb.
A force of 39 N is required to hold a spring stretched 0.3 m past equilibrium. Find $k$, and the work required to compress the spring 0.5 m from equilibrium.
$k=\frac{39}{0.3}$.
$W=\int_0^{-0.5}kx\,dx=\frac12k(0.5)^2$.
$k=130$ N/m and $W=65\cdot0.25=16.25$ J. A stretch and a compression of the same size take the same work.

Mastery check

No hints are offered until you've committed to an answer. This checks recognition, understanding, unfamiliar problems, and whether you can explain why.

Recognition. Which of these need an integral, rather than just $W=Fd$? Select all that apply.
Why

A chain on a roof and a tank: different pieces rise different distances (slice the object). A spring and a leaky bucket: the force changes along the path (∫F dx). The box and the helicopter rope move as a whole under a constant force, so $W=Fd$ (notes p. 15).

Understanding. Two identical cones, radius 5 m and height 12 m, are full of water and emptied over their tops. One has its vertex down (notes p. 18), the other its vertex up. Which takes more work?
You lift a bucket of sand 120 ft at a constant 2 ft/s. It starts with 50 lb of sand and leaks 1 lb per second, until only 1 lb of sand is left, which then stays in the bucket. How much work is done lifting the sand? (Ignore the bucket and the rope.)
$F=50-t$ lb and $y=2t$, so $F=50-\frac y2$, until $F=1$.
$50-\frac y2=1$ at $y=98$ ft. After that, $F=1$ lb for the last 22 ft.
$W=\int_0^{98}\big(50-\frac y2\big)\,dy+\int_{98}^{120}1\,dy=\big(4900-2401\big)+22=2521$ ft-lb. A force given in pieces gives an integral in pieces, like splitting $|v|$ at a turning point (§3.1).
Calculate the work done by the force $F(x)=\dfrac{x^2+x+1}{x+1}$ newtons on a particle as it moves from $x=0$ to $x=4$ meters.
The top's degree is at least the bottom's, so divide first (§1.3): $\frac{x^2+x+1}{x+1}=x+\frac1{x+1}$.
$W=\int_0^4\Big(x+\frac1{x+1}\Big)\,dx=\Big[\frac{x^2}2+\ln|x+1|\Big]_0^4=8+\ln5\approx9.609$ J.

Explain why (Level 7)

Why does pulling a 50 ft hanging rope onto a roof take half the work of lifting the same rope 50 ft with a helicopter? Explain with slices, and check it with the center of mass.

A model explanation

$W=Fd$ only works when every part of the object moves the same distance under the same force. With the helicopter, the whole rope rises 50 ft together, so $W=100\cdot50=5000$ ft-lb (notes p. 15). When the rope is pulled onto the roof, different pieces travel different distances: the piece at height $y$ only has $50-y$ ft to go. So cut the rope into thin pieces. Each one weighs $2\Delta y$ lb and rises $50-y$ ft, so its work is $2(50-y)\Delta y$. Adding them up gives $\int_0^{50}2(50-y)\,dy=2500$ ft-lb (notes p. 16). The bottom piece goes 50 ft, the top piece almost 0, and on average a piece goes 25 ft: half. The center of mass says the same thing: the rope's center of mass starts 25 ft below the roof and ends on it, so $W=(\text{weight})\times(\text{rise of the center of mass})=100\times25=2500$ ft-lb.
