Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

Velocity is the rate of change of position: $v(t)=p'(t)$. By the FTC, adding up $v(t)\,dt$ over a time interval gives the net change in position, the displacement. Moving forward adds and moving backward subtracts.

An odometer, though, doesn't run backward when you reverse. Total distance traveled adds up the speed $|v(t)|$, so every stretch counts positively. Notes p. 41: 10 ft/s forward for 30 s, then 10 ft/s back for 30 s gives a displacement of 0 ft but a distance of 600 ft.

What to notice: this is the velocity graph from notes p. 41. The two rectangles have the same size but opposite signs. The signed areas cancel (displacement 0); the actual areas add (distance 600 ft).

It's the FTC (§2.4) again, applied to motion

Prerequisite map

  • Displacement & distance

    • The FTC: $\int_a^b v\,dt=p(b)-p(a)$ (§2.4)
    • Solving $v(t)=0$: where the object can turn around

      show$(t-1)(t-3)=0$, so $t=1,3$

Why it works

This is the FTC with $f=v$ and $F=p$, since position is an antiderivative of velocity. On the velocity graph:

  • Displacement = signed area between the $v$-graph and the $t$-axis.
  • Distance = actual area, with every piece counted positively.

Recognition: which integral does the question want?

The question says…Use…
“displacement,” “net change in position,” “where is it at time $b$” (given the start)$\int v\,dt$, plus the starting position if it asks where
“total distance traveled,” “how far did it travel,” “odometer”$\int|v|\,dt$: find where $v=0$, split, make each piece positive
velocity given only as a tableA numerical rule on the data (§2.6)
$v$ never changes sign on $[a,b]$distance $=|\text{displacement}|$, no splitting needed
Tempting alternatives, and why to reject them
  • “Distance $=\left|\int_a^b v\,dt\right|$.” The absolute value belongs inside the integral. Outside, cancellations have already happened.
  • “Split where $p(t)=0$.” $p=0$ just means passing the starting point or origin. Direction changes happen where $v=0$.

Stage 4 · Attempt

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

An object moves with velocity $v(t)=t^2-4t+3$ m/s for $0\le t\le4$. Find its displacement and its total distance traveled.

Step 1

$\left[\tfrac{t^3}{3}-2t^2+3t\right]_0^4=\tfrac{64}{3}-32+12=\tfrac43$ m. No splitting needed for displacement.
Step 2
When does the object turn around?
$v=(t-1)(t-3)=0$ at $t=1$ and $t=3$. Direction can only change where $v=0$.
Step 3
On $(1,3)$, the object is moving…
$v(2)=-1<0$: backward on $(1,3)$, and forward on $(0,1)$ and $(3,4)$.
Step 4

$F(1)-F(0)=\left(\tfrac13-2+3\right)-0=\tfrac43$, where $F(t)=\tfrac{t^3}{3}-2t^2+3t$.
Step 5

$F(3)-F(1)=(9-18+9)-\tfrac43=-\tfrac43$. It moved backward $\tfrac43$ m, which contributes $+\tfrac43$ to the distance.
Step 6

$F(4)-F(3)=\tfrac43-0=\tfrac43$.
Step 7

$\tfrac43+\tfrac43+\tfrac43=4$ m, versus a displacement of $\tfrac43$ m. It went forward $\tfrac43$, back $\tfrac43$, then forward $\tfrac43$ again.
Takeaway. Displacement is one integral. Distance means splitting at the zeros of $v$, then adding the absolute values of the pieces. It's the same move as total area in §2.4.
A particle moves with velocity $v(t)=6-2t$ m/s for $0\le t\le5$. Find (a) its displacement and (b) its total distance traveled.
  1. Which quantity needs splitting, and which doesn't?
  2. When is $v(t)=0$? What's the sign of $v$ on each side?
Displacement needs no splitting. For distance, find when $v(t)=6-2t=0$.
$t=3$. $v>0$ before it and $v<0$ after. Split $\int_0^5|v|\,dt=\int_0^3(6-2t)\,dt+\int_3^5(2t-6)\,dt$.
$\int_0^3(6-2t)\,dt=9$ and $\int_3^5(6-2t)\,dt=-4$.
(a) $\big[6t-t^2\big]_0^5=30-25=5$ m. (b) $9+|-4|=13$ m. It went 9 m forward, then 4 m back: net 5 m, total 13 m.

Stage 5 · Compare

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

Spot the error. A student finds the distance traveled for $v(t)=t^2-4t+3$ on $[0,4]$:

What went wrong

The absolute value must go inside: distance $=\int_0^4|v(t)|\,dt=4$ m. By the time you take $|\,\cdot\,|$ of the whole integral, the backward motion has already cancelled some of the forward motion. The textbook warns (Example 3.1.5) that you definitely don't find an antiderivative of $v$ and then take its absolute value.

✗ Tempting
“$p(t)=0$ at $t=0$ and $t=3$, so I'll split at those times.”
✓ Correct
The pieces must be where the direction is constant, so split where $v(t)=0$. Passing through the origin ($p=0$) doesn't change direction.
Clue for next time: “turns around” means $v=0$ and $v$ changes sign.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Giving displacement when distance is asked

Why it happens
$\int v\,dt$ is the natural first computation.

Prevent it
Circle the word in the question. “Distance” means $|v|$ and a split.

Missing a turning point

Why it happens
Trig velocities can have several zeros. $\sin3t$ is 0 at both $\frac\pi3$ and $\frac{2\pi}3$ inside $(0,\pi)$.

Prevent it
Solve $v=0$ over the whole interval, then test the sign on each piece. (Even computer algebra can miss these: our own verification script did, until it was fixed.)

Forgetting the starting position

Why it happens
Displacement and position blur together.

Prevent it
“Where is it at time $b$?” means $p(b)=p(a)+\int_a^bv\,dt$.

Units

Why it happens
Integrating a rate changes the units.

Prevent it
$\text{(ft/s)}\times\text{s}=\text{ft}$: the answer is a length, not a speed.

Stage 6 · Practice

Practice

An object moves at 8 ft/s for 15 s, then in the opposite direction at 4 ft/s for 10 s. Find (a) its displacement and (b) its total distance traveled.
Sketch $v$: 8 on $[0,15)$ and $-4$ on $[15,25]$. Each integral is the area of a rectangle.
(a) $8\cdot15+(-4)\cdot10=120-40=80$ ft. (b) $120+40=160$ ft.
$v(t)=t^2-9$ on $[0,5]$. Find (a) the displacement and (b) the distance traveled.
$v=0$ at $t=3$ (in the interval). $v<0$ before and $v>0$ after.
$\int_0^3(9-t^2)\,dt=18$ and $\int_3^5(t^2-9)\,dt=\tfrac{44}{3}$.
(a) $\left[\tfrac{t^3}3-9t\right]_0^5=\tfrac{125}3-45=-\tfrac{10}3$. (b) $18+\tfrac{44}3=\tfrac{98}3\approx32.67$.
$v(t)=t^2-5t+6$ on $[0,3]$. Find (a) the displacement and (b) the total distance traveled.
$v=(t-2)(t-3)$: zero at $t=2$ (inside) and $t=3$ (the endpoint).
$\int_0^2v\,dt=\tfrac{14}3$ and $\int_2^3v\,dt=-\tfrac16$.
(a) $\left[\tfrac{t^3}3-\tfrac52t^2+6t\right]_0^3=9-22.5+18=\tfrac92$. (b) $\tfrac{14}3+\tfrac16=\tfrac{29}6\approx4.833$.
$v(t)=\sin3t$ on $[0,\pi]$. Find (a) the displacement and (b) the total distance traveled. (Count the turning points carefully!)
$\sin3t=0$ when $3t=\pi,2\pi$, so at $t=\frac\pi3$ and $\frac{2\pi}3$: two turning points.
Each hump has area $\int_0^{\pi/3}\sin3t\,dt=\left[-\tfrac{\cos3t}{3}\right]_0^{\pi/3}=\tfrac23$, and there are three humps.
(a) $\left[-\tfrac{\cos3t}{3}\right]_0^{\pi}=\tfrac{1+1}{3}=\tfrac23$ (up, down, up: net one hump). (b) $3\cdot\tfrac23=2$.
An object starts at position $p(0)=10$ with $v(t)=2t-6$ for $0\le t\le4$. Find (a) its position at $t=4$ and (b) its total distance traveled.
$p(4)=p(0)+\int_0^4v\,dt$.
$v=0$ at $t=3$: $\int_0^3(6-2t)\,dt=9$ and $\int_3^4(2t-6)\,dt=1$.
(a) $10+\big[t^2-6t\big]_0^4=10-8=2$. (b) $9+1=10$.
$v(t)=3t^2-12t+8$ m/s on $[2,4]$. The displacement is 0 (textbook Example 3.1.2). Find the distance traveled.
Solve $3t^2-12t+8=0$ with the quadratic formula. Which root is in $[2,4]$?
$t=2+\frac{2\sqrt3}{3}\approx3.155$. Here $v(2)=-4<0$ and $v(4)=8>0$. An antiderivative is $F(t)=t(t-2)(t-4)$.
With $c=2+\frac{2\sqrt3}{3}$: distance $=-\big(F(c)-F(2)\big)+\big(F(4)-F(c)\big)=-2F(c)=\frac{32\sqrt3}{9}\approx6.158$ m (since $F(2)=F(4)=0$).
A particle moving along a line has $v(t)=e^t-2$ for $0\le t\le1$. Find (a) the displacement and (b) the total distance traveled.
(a) An antiderivative of $e^t-2$ is $e^t-2t$.
(b) Solve $e^t-2=0$: $t=\ln2$. Is it inside $[0,1]$? The professor checks $e^0=1<2<e^1$. Test a point on each side.
$v<0$ on $(0,\ln2)$ and $v>0$ on $(\ln2,1)$. So distance $=\int_0^{\ln2}(2-e^t)\,dt+\int_{\ln2}^1(e^t-2)\,dt$.
(a) $\big[e^t-2t\big]_0^1=(e-2)-1=e-3\approx-0.282$. (b) $\big[2t-e^t\big]_0^{\ln2}+\big[e^t-2t\big]_{\ln2}^1=(2\ln2-1)+\big(e-4+2\ln2\big)=4\ln2+e-5\approx0.491$. This is the professor’s work on new notes p. 1 (verified by hand, SymPy and Wolfram).
A particle moving along a line has $v(t)=t^2-t-2$. Find (a) the displacement and (b) the total distance traveled on $0\le t\le4$.
Factor: $t^2-t-2=(t-2)(t+1)$. Which root lies in $[0,4]$?
Only $t=2$. Test points: $t=1$ gives $-2<0$ and $t=3$ gives $4>0$.
Distance $=\int_0^2(-t^2+t+2)\,dt+\int_2^4(t^2-t-2)\,dt$.
(a) $\big[\tfrac{t^3}{3}-\tfrac{t^2}{2}-2t\big]_0^4=\tfrac{64}{3}-8-8=\tfrac{16}{3}$. (b) $\tfrac{10}{3}+\tfrac{26}{3}=12$ (new notes p. 2).
A particle moving along a line has $v(t)=1-2\cos t$. Find (a) the displacement and (b) the total distance traveled on $0\le t\le\pi$.
(a) An antiderivative is $t-2\sin t$, and $\sin\pi=\sin0=0$.
(b) $1-2\cos t=0$ means $\cos t=\tfrac12$, so $t=\tfrac\pi3$ in $[0,\pi]$. The sign is $-$ before and $+$ after.
Distance $=\int_0^{\pi/3}(2\cos t-1)\,dt+\int_{\pi/3}^{\pi}(1-2\cos t)\,dt$.
(a) $\big[t-2\sin t\big]_0^\pi=\pi$. (b) $\big[2\sin t-t\big]_0^{\pi/3}+\big[t-2\sin t\big]_{\pi/3}^{\pi}=2\sin\tfrac\pi3-\tfrac\pi3+\pi-\tfrac\pi3+2\sin\tfrac\pi3=2\sqrt3+\tfrac\pi3\approx4.511$ (new notes p. 3).
Stage 7 · Master

Mastery check

If $v(t)>0$ for every $t$ in $[a,b]$, the distance traveled is ___ the displacement.
Which of these can be negative? Select all that apply.
Why

Velocity and displacement carry a direction. Speed $=|v|$ and distance $=\int|v|$ never go below 0.

$v(t)=\sin t+\cos t$ on $[0,2\pi]$. Find the total distance traveled.
$\sin t+\cos t=0$ means $\tan t=-1$. Which $t$ in $[0,2\pi]$?
$t=\frac{3\pi}4$ and $\frac{7\pi}4$. An antiderivative is $-\cos t+\sin t$. Integrate over the three pieces.
The pieces are $\sqrt2+1$, $-2\sqrt2$, and $\sqrt2-1$ (check these!), so distance $=(\sqrt2+1)+2\sqrt2+(\sqrt2-1)=4\sqrt2\approx5.657$. (Slicker: $\sin t+\cos t=\sqrt2\sin(t+\frac\pi4)$, and $|\sin|$ over a full period has area 4.)
A particle has velocity $v(t)=e^{-t}$. What is the limit, as $t_1\to\infty$, of the distance it travels between $t=0$ and $t=t_1$? (It never stops moving. Does it go infinitely far?)
$v>0$ always, so distance = displacement $=\int_0^{t_1}e^{-t}\,dt$.
That's $1-e^{-t_1}$. Now take the limit (§2.5, Type I).
$\lim_{t_1\to\infty}\big(1-e^{-t_1}\big)=1$. It moves forever but covers only 1 unit in total: an infinitely long time with finite distance, the same idea as an infinitely long region with finite area.
A particle has $v(t)=e^t-3$ for $0\le t\le1$. Find the total distance traveled. (Compare new notes p. 1, where $v=e^t-2$.)
Where is $e^t-3=0$? Is that time inside $[0,1]$?
$t=\ln3\approx1.099>1$: no turning point inside. What is the sign of $v$ on the whole interval?
$v(0)=-2$ and $v(1)=e-3<0$, so $v<0$ throughout and distance $=-\int_0^1(e^t-3)\,dt=-\big[(e-3)-(1-0)\big]=4-e\approx1.282$. The professor’s check $e^0<2<e^1$ is what decides whether to split. Here $3>e^1$, so there’s nothing to split.

Explain why (Level 7)

Give an example where $\left|\int_a^b v(t)\,dt\right|\neq\int_a^b|v(t)|\,dt$, and explain in general when the two are equal. (Textbook §3.1 Additional Ex. 12.)

A model explanation

Notes p. 41: $v=10$ for 30 s, then $-10$ for 30 s. Then $\left|\int_0^{60}v\,dt\right|=|300-300|=0$ but $\int_0^{60}|v|\,dt=600$. They differ whenever $v$ changes sign, because the integral lets forward and backward motion cancel before the absolute value is taken. They're equal exactly when $v$ keeps one sign ($\ge0$ or $\le0$) on the whole interval, since then there's nothing to cancel.
