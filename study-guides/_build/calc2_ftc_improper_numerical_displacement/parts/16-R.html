Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

When you add fractions, you build a common denominator: $\frac{2}{x+1}-\frac{1}{x-3}=\frac{x-7}{(x+1)(x-3)}$. Partial fractions runs that backwards. It takes the complicated fraction and recovers the simple pieces that were added to make it. The professor's definition (notes p. 13): “write a complicated fraction as a sum of simpler fractions.”

Why bother? Each simple piece has an easy antiderivative: $\ln|x-a|$, $-\frac{1}{x-a}$, $\ln(x^2+c^2)$, or $\tan^{-1}\frac xc$. So $\int\frac{x-7}{x^2-2x-3}\,dx=2\ln|x+1|-\ln|x-3|+C$.

The same idea with numbers

$\frac12+\frac13=\frac56$ is easy. Going backwards, from $\frac56$ to “something over 2 plus something over 3,” is partial fractions. The denominator's factors tell you which pieces to look for; the numerators are the unknowns.

Three questions to ask every time
  1. Is the top's degree less than the bottom's? If not, do long division first (notes p. 19).
  2. How does the bottom factor? Distinct linear, repeated linear, or irreducible quadratic ($b^2-4ac<0$)?
  3. Can I integrate every piece? $\ln$, a power, $\tan^{-1}$, or a quick substitution.

Why the templates look the way they do

  • Each factor of the denominator can only come from adding fractions whose denominators are that factor (or its powers).
  • Over a linear factor the numerator is a constant; over a quadratic it's linear ($Bx+C$). Each numerator is one degree lower than its denominator.
  • The number of unknowns always equals the degree of $Q$. So matching coefficients gives exactly as many equations as unknowns.

Condition (notes p. 13)$f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ with $\deg P<\deg Q$. If $\deg P\ge\deg Q$: long division first (notes p. 19).
Integrating the pieces$\int\frac{A}{x-a}=A\ln|x-a|$; $\int\frac{B}{(x-b)^2}=-\frac{B}{x-b}$; $\int\frac{Dx}{x^2+c^2}=\frac D2\ln(x^2+c^2)$; $\int\frac{E}{x^2+c^2}=\frac Ec\tan^{-1}\frac xc$.

The professor's procedure (notes pp. 13–20)

  1. Compare degrees. If $\deg P\ge\deg Q$, long-divide: $\frac PQ=(\text{quotient})+\frac{\text{remainder}}{Q}$.
  2. Factor $Q$ completely: linear factors, repeated factors, irreducible quadratics.
  3. Write the template from the table, with unknown constants.
  4. Multiply through by $Q$, expand, and match the coefficients of $x^2$, $x$, and the constant.
  5. Solve the system, numbering the equations ①②③ and eliminating, as the professor does.
  6. Integrate each piece.

Example (notes p. 14): $\frac{5x+5}{(x-3)(x+2)}=\frac{A}{x-3}+\frac{B}{x+2}$ gives $5x+5=(A+B)x+(2A-3B)$. So ① $A+B=5$ and ② $2A-3B=5$. Then ①×3 + ②: $5A=20$, so $A=4$ and $B=1$.

Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“$\int\frac{x-7}{x^2-2x-3}\,dx=\ln|x^2-2x-3|+C$.” That's the “1 over anything” trap (textbook Remark 1.1.5). It would only be right if the numerator were the derivative of the denominator, $2x-2$. It isn't, so decompose.

What to notice
  1. With every piece switched on, the dashed sum lies exactly on the wide gray curve. A decomposition is an identity, not an approximation.
  2. In Notes p. 13: (x − 7)/(x² − 2x − 3), near $x=-1$ only $\frac{2}{x+1}$ blows up, and near $x=3$ only $-\frac{1}{x-3}$ does. Each vertical asymptote belongs to one factor of the denominator.
  3. Switch one piece off: the dashed sum peels away from $f$. Every term in the template is needed.
  4. In Notes p. 15: repeated factor x(x + 2)², switch off $-\frac{1}{(x+2)^2}$. The sum then misbehaves near $x=-2$. A repeated factor needs a term for each power.
  5. In Notes p. 20: long division first, far from the asymptotes $f$ hugs the line $-2x+4$. Simple fractions all die out as $x$ grows, so they can't produce that line. That's why the quotient from long division has to come first.
Which template fits $\dfrac{x^2-3x-8}{x(x+2)^2}$ (notes p. 15)? Try each and see what matching gives.
$x^2-3x-8=A(x+2)^2+Bx(x+2)$ gives three equations ($x^2$: $A+B=1$; $x$: $4A+2B=-3$; constant: $4A=-8$) but only two unknowns. $A=-2$ and $B=3$, and then $4A+2B=-2\ne-3$. No solution: the template is missing a term.
$x^2-3x-8=A(x+2)^2+Bx(x+2)+Cx$. Constant: $4A=-8$, so $A=-2$. $x^2$: $A+B=1$, so $B=3$. $x$: $4A+2B+C=-3$, so $C=-1$. This is the notes' answer, and it integrates to $-2\ln|x|+3\ln|x+2|+\frac{1}{x+2}+C$.
This works too, since $\frac{Bx+C}{(x+2)^2}=\frac{B}{x+2}+\frac{C-2B}{(x+2)^2}$. But you'd have to split it apart again to integrate. The table's form, one constant per power, is ready to integrate.
And for $\dfrac{x^2+5x-4}{x(x^2+4)}$ (notes p. 16)?
$x^2+5x-4=A(x^2+4)+Bx$ forces $A=1$ (from $x^2$) but also $4A=-4$ (from the constant). That's a contradiction: a constant over a quadratic isn't enough.
$x^2+5x-4=A(x^2+4)+(Bx+C)x$ gives $A=-1$, $B=2$, $C=5$. Then $\int\big(-\frac1x+\frac{2x}{x^2+4}+\frac{5}{x^2+4}\big)dx=-\ln|x|+\ln(x^2+4)+\frac52\tan^{-1}\frac x2+C$ (notes p. 16).
$x^2+4$ doesn't factor over the reals: $b^2-4ac=-16<0$. It's $x^2-4$ that factors. The notes' side remark: “$x^2-1=(x+1)(x-1)$, but $x^2+1$ is irreducible.”

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{11x+26}{x^2+5x+6}\,dx$ (textbook §1.3 Suggested Ex. 1), following the professor's procedure.

Step 1 · Degrees
Do you need long division first?
The top has degree 1 and the bottom degree 2: a proper fraction, ready to decompose.
Step 2 · Factor and template
What's the template?
$x^2+5x+6=(x+2)(x+3)$: two distinct linear factors. Multiplying through gives $11x+26=A(x+3)+B(x+2)=(A+B)x+(3A+2B)$.
Step 3 · Match coefficients

① $A+B=11$ (the $x$ terms) and ② $3A+2B=26$ (the constants). Eliminate $B$ with ② − 2×①.

② − 2×①: $(3A+2B)-(2A+2B)=26-22$, so $A=4$.
Step 4 · Back-substitute

From ①: $B=11-4=7$. Check ②: $3(4)+2(7)=26$ ✓.
Step 5 · Integrate the pieces

$4\ln|x+2|+7\ln|x+3|+C$.
Step 6 · Check
What's the fastest check that $A=4$, $B=7$ is right?
Adding the pieces back over the common denominator must return the original numerator. It's the definition of the decomposition, used as a check.
Takeaway. Degrees → factor → template → multiply out → match coefficients ①② → eliminate → integrate each piece. Recombine to check.
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{t+58}{t^2+6t-16}\,dt.$
  1. Compare the degrees. Is long division needed?
  2. How does $t^2+6t-16$ factor?
  3. After you multiply out, which coefficients do you match?
$t^2+6t-16=(t+8)(t-2)$. Template: $\frac{A}{t+8}+\frac{B}{t-2}$.
$t+58=A(t-2)+B(t+8)=(A+B)t+(-2A+8B)$. So ① $A+B=1$ and ② $-2A+8B=58$.
② + 2×①: $10B=60$.
  1. $B=6$, so $A=1-6=-5$.
  2. $\int\Big(\frac{-5}{t+8}+\frac{6}{t-2}\Big)dt=-5\ln|t+8|+6\ln|t-2|+C.$

Check: $\frac{-5(t-2)+6(t+8)}{(t+8)(t-2)}=\frac{t+58}{t^2+6t-16}$ ✓.

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

✗ Tempting
“$\frac{2x^2}{x^2-9}=\frac{A}{x-3}+\frac{B}{x+3}$.” Then $2x^2=A(x+3)+B(x-3)$, which has no $x^2$ on the right. Stuck.
✓ Correct
The degrees are equal, so divide first (notes p. 23): $\frac{2x^2}{x^2-9}=2+\frac{18}{x^2-9}=2+\frac{3}{x-3}-\frac{3}{x+3}$. The integral is $2x+3\ln|x-3|-3\ln|x+3|+C$.
Clue for next time: the degree comparison comes first, every time. The notes flag it in red: “deg of top = deg of bottom.”

A repeated factor $(x-2)^2$ needs a term for each power: $\frac Ax+\frac{B}{x-2}+\frac{C}{(x-2)^2}$ (notes p. 14). With that template, $A=2$, $B=-2$, $C=7$, and the integral is $2\ln|x|-2\ln|x-2|-\frac{7}{x-2}+C$. A contradiction while matching is the signal that a term is missing.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Skipping long division

Why it happens
The fraction looks ready to split.

Prevent it
Compare degrees before anything else (notes p. 19, red). If $\deg P\ge\deg Q$, divide.

“Factoring” $x^2+c^2$

Why it happens
$x^2-c^2=(x-c)(x+c)$ is so familiar that the plus sign gets ignored.

Prevent it
Check $b^2-4ac$. If it's negative, the quadratic stays whole with a linear numerator $Bx+C$.

One term for a repeated factor

Why it happens
A factor appears once in the factorization, so it seems to deserve one fraction.

Prevent it
$(x+2)^2$ contributes $\frac{B}{x+2}+\frac{C}{(x+2)^2}$. If the matching equations contradict each other, a term is missing.

Integrating $\frac{C}{(x+2)^2}$ as a logarithm

Why it happens
Every other piece gave a log.

Prevent it
It's the power rule: $\int C(x+2)^{-2}dx=-\frac{C}{x+2}$ (notes p. 15 gets $+\frac{1}{x+2}$ from $C=-1$).

Losing the $\tfrac12$ or the $\tfrac1a$ with a quadratic

Why it happens
Two different rules act on one piece.

Prevent it
Split $\frac{Dx+E}{x^2+c^2}$ into $\frac{Dx}{x^2+c^2}$ (substitution: $\frac D2\ln(x^2+c^2)$) and $\frac{E}{x^2+c^2}$ (arctan: $\frac Ec\tan^{-1}\frac xc$), as on notes p. 16.

Practice: standard applications

Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{x-7}{x^2-2x-3}\,dx.$
$x^2-2x-3=(x+1)(x-3)$. Template $\frac{A}{x+1}+\frac{B}{x-3}$.
$x-7=(A+B)x+(-3A+B)$: ① $A+B=1$, ② $-3A+B=-7$.
① − ②: $4A=8$, so $A=2$ and $B=-1$. The integral is $2\ln|x+1|-\ln|x-3|+C$ (notes p. 13).
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{x^2-3x-8}{x^3+4x^2+4x}\,dx.$
$x^3+4x^2+4x=x(x+2)^2$. Template: $\frac Ax+\frac{B}{x+2}+\frac{C}{(x+2)^2}$.
You should find $A=-2$, $B=3$, $C=-1$.
$\int\Big(-\frac2x+\frac{3}{x+2}-\frac{1}{(x+2)^2}\Big)dx=-2\ln|x|+3\ln|x+2|+\frac{1}{x+2}+C$ (notes p. 15).
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{3x^2+x-5}{(x+2)(x^2+1)}\,dx.$
Template $\frac{A}{x+2}+\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+1}$. Matching gives $A+B=3$, $2B+C=1$, $A+2C=-5$.
$A=1$, $B=2$, $C=-3$. Split $\frac{2x-3}{x^2+1}$ into $\frac{2x}{x^2+1}-\frac{3}{x^2+1}$.
$\ln|x+2|+\ln(x^2+1)-3\tan^{-1}x+C$ (notes p. 17).
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{3r}{r^4+5r^2+4}\,dr.$ Treat the bottom as a quadratic in $r^2$.
$r^4+5r^2+4=(r^2+1)(r^2+4)$. Template $\frac{Ar+B}{r^2+1}+\frac{Cr+D}{r^2+4}$.
Matching gives $B=D=0$, $A=1$, $C=-1$. Each piece is a substitution.
$\int\Big(\frac{r}{r^2+1}-\frac{r}{r^2+4}\Big)dr=\tfrac12\ln(r^2+1)-\tfrac12\ln(r^2+4)+C$.

Notes p. 18 has two cosmetic slips that don't affect the answer: the numerators are labeled $Ax+B$, $Cx+D$ though the variable is $r$, and “④ − ②” should read “④ − ③”.

Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{t^4+6t^2+11}{t^2+4}\,dt.$
Degree 4 ≥ 2: divide. $t^4+6t^2+11=(t^2+4)(t^2+2)+3$.
So the integrand is $t^2+2+\frac{3}{t^2+4}$. Use $\int\frac{dt}{a^2+t^2}=\frac1a\tan^{-1}\frac ta$.
$\tfrac13t^3+2t+\tfrac32\tan^{-1}\tfrac t2+C$ (notes p. 19).
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{4y-2}{(y-1)(y^2+1)}\,dy.$
Template $\frac{A}{y-1}+\frac{By+C}{y^2+1}$: $4y-2=(A+B)y^2+(C-B)y+(A-C)$.
① $A+B=0$, ② $C-B=4$, ③ $A-C=-2$. Adding all three: $2A=2$.
$A=1$, $B=-1$, $C=3$: $\int\Big(\frac{1}{y-1}+\frac{-y+3}{y^2+1}\Big)dy=\ln|y-1|-\tfrac12\ln(y^2+1)+3\tan^{-1}y+C$.
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{2j^2+18j+43}{(j+5)^3}\,dj.$
A cubed factor gets three terms: $\frac{A}{j+5}+\frac{B}{(j+5)^2}+\frac{C}{(j+5)^3}$ (notes p. 14).
$2j^2+18j+43=A(j+5)^2+B(j+5)+C$ gives $A=2$, $B=-2$, $C=3$.
$\int\Big(\frac{2}{j+5}-\frac{2}{(j+5)^2}+\frac{3}{(j+5)^3}\Big)dj=2\ln|j+5|+\frac{2}{j+5}-\frac{3}{2(j+5)^2}+C$.
Find $\displaystyle\int\frac{x^3+12x^2+51x+74}{x^2+8x+15}\,dx.$
Divide: quotient $x+4$, remainder $4x+14$.
$\frac{4x+14}{(x+3)(x+5)}=\frac{A}{x+3}+\frac{B}{x+5}$ with $A=1$, $B=3$.
$\int\Big(x+4+\frac{1}{x+3}+\frac{3}{x+5}\Big)dx=\frac{x^2}{2}+4x+\ln|x+3|+3\ln|x+5|+C$.
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^2+3x+2}.$
$\frac{1}{(x+1)(x+2)}=\frac{1}{x+1}-\frac{1}{x+2}$.
The antiderivative is $\ln|x+1|-\ln|x+2|$. Evaluate from 0 to 1.
$(\ln2-\ln3)-(\ln1-\ln2)=2\ln2-\ln3=\ln\frac43\approx0.2877$.

Explain why (Level 7)

In your own words: why must the top's degree be lower than the bottom's before you decompose, and what does long division accomplish?

A model explanation

Every simple fraction in the table ($\frac{A}{x-a}$, $\frac{B}{(x-b)^2}$, $\frac{Dx+E}{x^2+c^2}$) goes to 0 as $x\to\pm\infty$, and so does any sum of them. So a sum of simple fractions can only equal a fraction that also dies out, which means one with $\deg P<\deg Q$. If $\deg P\ge\deg Q$, the fraction grows or levels off like a polynomial (notes p. 20 hugs the line $-2x+4$). Long division peels off that polynomial part, the quotient, and leaves a proper remainder fraction, which can be decomposed. Also, the number of unknowns in the template equals $\deg Q$, which is exactly the number of coefficients to match when $\deg P<\deg Q$.
