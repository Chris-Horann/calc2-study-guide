Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

Your notes ask (p. 27): “What if we have some ‘infinity’ in the area under the curve?” The region might be infinitely long (the interval runs off to ∞) or infinitely tall (the graph shoots up at a point). You can't plug ∞ into $F$. So do what the notes do: stop at a finite stand-in, compute the area up to there, then let the stand-in move toward the problem and watch what the areas do.

If the areas settle down to a number, the integral converges to that number. If they grow without bound, or never settle, it diverges.

The surprise: an infinitely long region can have finite area, if the curve thins out fast enough ($\int_1^\infty e^{-x}\,dx=e^{-1}$, notes p. 28). But getting thinner isn't enough by itself: $\frac1x\to0$, yet $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x}$ diverges.

What to notice: both shaded regions are infinite in extent, one to the right and one upward, yet each has a finite area.
Type I (notes p. 27)

“Infinity happens in the horizontal direction”: the domain of integration is unbounded, e.g. $\int_1^\infty e^{-x}\,dx$.

Type II (notes p. 30)

“Infinity happens in the vertical direction”: the integrand is unbounded, with an infinite discontinuity at some $x=c$, e.g. $\int_9^{10}3(x-9)^{-1/3}\,dx$.

Definition (notes p. 28)

An improper integral converges (is convergent) if the limit exists as a finite number, and diverges (is divergent) if the limit does not exist as a finite number.

Why this definition?

The ordinary integral is built from strips over a closed, bounded interval, and it can't handle an unbounded function or interval. The textbook (§2.5) points out that the Riemann integral of an unbounded function doesn't even exist. So we need a new definition, and there's really only one sensible choice:

The area over $[1,\infty)$ should be whatever the areas over $[1,b]$ approach as $b$ grows. Every one of those finite pieces is an ordinary integral, so you compute it with the FTC and take a limit last.

More than one “problematic point” notes p. 32 · Definition 2.5.11

Isolate the problems by splitting the integral so that each piece has exactly one problem, at one end. The integral converges if and only if every piece converges; it diverges as soon as one piece diverges. For $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}$, split at any convenient point (such as 0) and use two separate limits.

A pattern you can rebuild: $\int x^{-p}\,dx$ at ∞ and near 0

How to find hidden problem points: set denominators to 0; look for $(\text{something})^{\text{negative power}}$, $\ln(\text{something}\to0)$, or $\tan$ at $\tfrac\pi2$.

Tempting alternatives, and why to reject them
  • “Just plug in ∞.” ∞ isn't a number. Plugging it in can hide $\infty-\infty$ or an oscillation (like $\cos b$).
  • “The integrand goes to 0, so it converges.” False: $\frac1x\to0$ but $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x}=\infty$. How fast it goes to 0 is what matters.
  • “It's symmetric, so it cancels.” $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,dx$ is not 0. Each half diverges, so the integral diverges.

Stage 4 · Attempt

Guided example, then your turn

Guided example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^3\frac{dx}{\sqrt[3]{(x-1)^2}}$ if it converges.

Step 1
Is this integral proper or improper?
At $x=1$ the denominator is $\sqrt[3]{0}=0$, so the integrand $\to+\infty$ from both sides. A problem point inside the interval is the easiest kind to miss, because the limits look innocent.
Step 2
How should you split it?
Each piece must have its problem at exactly one end (notes p. 32): $\int_0^1$ has it at the right end and $\int_1^3$ at the left end.
Step 3
An antiderivative of $(x-1)^{-2/3}$ is…
Power rule: $\frac{(x-1)^{1/3}}{1/3}=3(x-1)^{1/3}=3\sqrt[3]{x-1}$. Use the real cube root: the cube root of a negative number is negative.
Step 4

$\lim_{b\to1^-}3\sqrt[3]{b-1}-3\sqrt[3]{-1}=0-(-3)=3.$ The limit exists, so this piece converges.
Step 5

$3\sqrt[3]{2}-\lim_{a\to1^+}3\sqrt[3]{a-1}=3\sqrt[3]2-0\approx3.780.$ This piece converges too.
Step 6

So the integral converges to:

$3+3\sqrt[3]{2}\approx6.780$. Both pieces converge, so the whole integral converges. This is the same pattern as textbook Example 2.5.9, $\int_{-1}^8x^{-2/3}\,dx=9$.
Takeaway. Scan for problem points before integrating. Split so each piece has one. Take a one-sided limit toward each problem, from inside. The integral converges only if every piece does.

$x=1$ is a problem point inside the interval. Split: $\int_0^1+\int_1^2$. The whole thing converges only if both pieces converge.
$\displaystyle\int_0^1=\lim_{b\to1^-}\left[-\frac{1}{x-1}\right]_0^b=\lim_{b\to1^-}\left(-\frac{1}{b-1}\right)-1.$ As $b\to1^-$, $b-1\to0^-$, so $-\frac1{b-1}\to\,?$

$-\frac{1}{b-1}\to+\infty$ as $b\to1^-$, so $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{(x-1)^2}$ diverges. One divergent piece is enough: the integral diverges (notes p. 32). You don't even need $\int_1^2$.

The trap: $F(2)-F(0)=\left[-\frac1{x-1}\right]_0^2=-1-1=-2$, a negative “answer” for a positive integrand. Always scan for problem points first.

Tempting approaches vs. correct reasoning

✗ Tempting
“$\frac1x\to0$ as $x\to\infty$, so $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x}$ converges.”
✓ Correct
$\int_1^b\frac{dx}{x}=\ln b\to\infty$, so it diverges. By contrast $\int_1^b\frac{dx}{x^2}=1-\frac1b\to1$.
Clue for next time: shrinking to 0 is necessary but not enough. Compute the limit; don't judge by the integrand alone.

Line 2 uses one symmetric limit. The definition splits the integral at a point, say 0, into $\int_{-\infty}^0x\,dx+\int_0^\infty x\,dx$, with separate limits. Since $\int_0^\infty x\,dx=\lim\frac{b^2}{2}=\infty$, one piece diverges, so the whole integral diverges (notes p. 32). The symmetric shortcut hides two infinite pieces cancelling.

✗ Tempting

✓ Correct
$\int_0^b\sin x\,dx=1-\cos b$ bounces between 0 and 2 forever, so there's no limit and the integral diverges (textbook §2.5 Suggested Ex. 3). “Diverges” means no finite limit, and that includes oscillating.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Missing an interior problem point

Why it happens
The limits look fine, so it's tempting to go straight to $F(b)-F(a)$.

Prevent it
Before any improper-or-not decision, set every denominator to 0 and check whether that $x$ lies in $[a,b]$.

Doing arithmetic with ∞

Why it happens
Shortcuts like “$e^{-\infty}=0$” feel natural.

Prevent it
Write the limit explicitly, $\lim_{b\to\infty}(\dots)$, as the notes do on every example. It catches $\infty-\infty$ and oscillation.

Using symmetry to cancel two infinite pieces

Why it happens
For proper integrals, odd symmetry really does give 0.

Prevent it
For improper integrals, first check that each piece converges on its own. Only then can you add them. (Mixed practice has one where symmetry does work, because each half converges.)

Approaching from the wrong side

Why it happens
The minus-or-plus superscript looks arbitrary.

Prevent it
Approach from inside the interval. A problem at the left end means $a\to c^+$; at the right end, $b\to c^-$.

Sign slips as something goes to 0

Why it happens
$\frac{1}{\text{small}}$ is huge, but is it $+$ or $-$?

Prevent it
Ask whether the small quantity is $0^+$ or $0^-$. Notes p. 31: $b\to1^-$ makes $b^2-1\to0^-$, so $(b^2-1)^{-1}\to-\infty$.

Thinking one convergent piece rescues a divergent one

Why it happens
“Part of it has a nice value.”

Prevent it
Notes p. 32: the integral converges if and only if all parts converge. $\int_0^1x^{-0.8}\,dx=5$ doesn't save $\int_1^\infty x^{-0.8}\,dx=\infty$.

Practice: evaluate or show divergence

For every problem, first name the type (I, II at an endpoint, or a problem inside) and where the limit goes. Type a number, or diverges.

$\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^3}$
Write it as $\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_1^bx^{-3}\,dx$.
An antiderivative is $-\frac{1}{2x^2}$. What happens to $-\frac{1}{2b^2}$ as $b\to\infty$?
$\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-\frac1{2b^2}+\frac12\right)=\frac12$. It converges ($p=3>1$).
$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{(2x+5)^2}$
Substitute $u=2x+5$ (or just guess and check). An antiderivative is $-\frac{1}{2(2x+5)}$.
$\lim_{b\to\infty}\left[-\frac{1}{2(2x+5)}\right]_0^b$. What are the two terms?
$\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2(2b+5)}\right)+\frac1{10}=0+\frac1{10}=\frac1{10}.$
$\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dy}{\sqrt{4y-3}}$
An antiderivative is $\frac12\sqrt{4y-3}$.
What does $\frac12\sqrt{4b-3}$ do as $b\to\infty$?
$\frac12\sqrt{4b-3}-\frac12\to\infty$, so it diverges. It behaves like $\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}$, which is $p=\frac12\le1$.
$\displaystyle\int_1^9\frac{2}{\sqrt[3]{x-1}}\,dx$
The problem is at $x=1$, the left end, so use $\lim_{a\to1^+}$.
An antiderivative is $2\cdot\frac32(x-1)^{2/3}=3(x-1)^{2/3}$.
$3\cdot8^{2/3}-\lim_{a\to1^+}3(a-1)^{2/3}=3\cdot4-0=12.$
$\displaystyle\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-t^2}}$
Where is the problem? (The denominator is 0 at $t=1$.) So use $\lim_{b\to1^-}$.
An antiderivative is $\sin^{-1}t$ (notes p. 1).
$\lim_{b\to1^-}\sin^{-1}b-\sin^{-1}\tfrac12=\frac\pi2-\frac\pi6=\frac\pi3.$
$\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{1+x^2}$
Split at 0: two Type I pieces, each with its own limit.
$\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\tan^{-1}b$. What is that limit?
Each half is $\frac\pi2$ (both converge), so the total is $\pi$.
$\displaystyle\int_3^\infty\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^2}$
Substitute $u=\ln x$, $du=\frac{dx}{x}$.
An antiderivative is $-\frac{1}{\ln x}$. What happens as $b\to\infty$?
$\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-\frac1{\ln b}\right)+\frac1{\ln3}=\frac1{\ln3}\approx0.910.$
$\displaystyle\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x\ln x}$. Compare it with Practice 7: only one power of $\ln x$ is missing.
Same substitution: $u=\ln x$.
An antiderivative is $\ln(\ln x)$. As $b\to\infty$?
$\ln(\ln b)\to\infty$ (very slowly), so it diverges. One power of $\ln x$ is the difference between converging and diverging.
$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-x^2}\,dx$
$u=-x^2$, $du=-2x\,dx$. An antiderivative is $-\frac12e^{-x^2}$.
$\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-\frac12e^{-b^2}\right)+\frac12=\frac12.$
$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{\tan^{-1}z}{1+z^2}\,dz$
$u=\tan^{-1}z$, $du=\frac{dz}{1+z^2}$. An antiderivative is $\frac12(\tan^{-1}z)^2$.
As $z\to\infty$, $\tan^{-1}z\to\frac\pi2$.
$\frac12\left(\frac\pi2\right)^2-0=\frac{\pi^2}{8}\approx1.234.$
$\displaystyle\int_3^4\frac{x}{(x^2-9)^3}\,dx$
The problem is at $x=3$, the left end: $\lim_{a\to3^+}$.
$u=x^2-9$ gives the antiderivative $-\frac{1}{4(x^2-9)^2}$.
$-\frac{1}{4\cdot49}+\lim_{a\to3^+}\frac{1}{4(a^2-9)^2}=+\infty$, so it diverges.

Explain why (Level 7)

How can a region that is infinitely long have a finite area? Use $\int_1^\infty e^{-x}\,dx$ or $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2}$, and contrast it with $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x}$.

A model explanation

“Area over $[1,\infty)$” means the limit of the areas over $[1,b]$. Each extra stretch of the region adds a little area. If the curve thins out fast enough, those additions shrink so quickly that the running total levels off: $\int_1^b\frac{dx}{x^2}=1-\frac1b\to1$. The total is like a sum whose terms shrink fast. For $\frac1x$ the thinning is too slow: $\int_1^b\frac{dx}{x}=\ln b$ keeps growing, because each doubling of $b$ adds the same amount, $\ln2$.
