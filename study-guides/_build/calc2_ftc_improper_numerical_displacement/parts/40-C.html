Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

Some integrals can't be done with antiderivatives. $e^{-x^2}$, $e^{x^2}$, and $\frac{1}{\ln x}$ have no elementary antiderivative (textbook §2.6). The integral still exists, though: it's an area. So we estimate it.

Cut $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal strips. On each strip, replace the curve by a shape whose area you know: flat tops (the Midpoint Rule), slanted straight tops (the Trapezoid Rule), or parabolic tops (Simpson's Rule). Shapes that hug the curve better leave a smaller error.

What to notice: the same curve and the same two strips, with three kinds of tops. Compare the gaps between each shape and the curve.
The setup every rule shares (notes p. 34)
$$\Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n},\qquad x_i=a+i\,\Delta x,\qquad a=x_0$n$ subintervals give $n+1$ grid points. The Midpoint Rule uses the $n$ midpoints instead.

Why the Trapezoid Rule has 2's

One trapezoid has area $\frac{\Delta x}{2}\big(f(x_{i-1})+f(x_i)\big)$. Add them up for $n=4$:

[f₀+f₁] + [f₁+f₂] + [f₂+f₃] + [f₃+f₄]
= f₀ + 2f₁ + 2f₂ + 2f₃ + f₄

Every interior point is a side shared by two trapezoids, so it's counted twice. Each endpoint belongs to only one. That's the whole story behind “Note the coefficients!” (notes p. 36).

Why Simpson's Rule is 1, 4, 2, 4, …, 4, 1 and needs $n$ even

Simpson's Rule fits one parabola over each pair of strips, through three points. Its area over $[x_{i-1},x_{i+1}]$ is $\frac{\Delta x}{3}\big(f_{i-1}+4f_i+f_{i+1}\big)$ (notes p. 38). Add the pairs for $n=4$:

[f₀ + 4f₁ + f₂] + [f₂ + 4f₃ + f₄] = f₀ + 4f₁ + 2f₂ + 4f₃ + f₄

Each pair's middle point gets a 4. A point shared by two neighbouring pairs gets $1+1=2$. Because the strips are used in pairs, $n$ must be even; with $n=5$ one strip is left over (notes p. 38).

Where does $\frac{\Delta x}{3}(y_0+4y_1+y_2)$ come from? (a two-line derivation)

Shift so the middle point is at $x=0$ and write $h=\Delta x$. The parabola is $y=Ax^2+Bx+C$ on $[-h,h]$. Its area is

$$\int_{-h}^{h}(Ax^2+Bx+C)\,dx=\tfrac23Ah^3+2Ch=\tfrac h3\big(2Ah^2+6C\big).$$

The three heights are $y_0=Ah^2-Bh+C$, $y_1=C$, $y_2=Ah^2+Bh+C$, so $y_0+4y_1+y_2=2Ah^2+6C$, the same bracket. Therefore the area is $\frac h3\,(y_0+4y_1+y_2)$. The $B$ terms cancel, and that is where the “4” comes from.

What to notice

  2. Double $n$ and watch the error table. For $e^{-x^2}$ the Midpoint and Trapezoid errors shrink about 4×, while Simpson's shrinks about 16× once $n\ge4$.
  3. Switch to $\sqrt{1-x^2}$: every rule improves much more slowly (only about 2.8× per doubling), and at $n=4$ even the Midpoint Rule beats Simpson's. The curve has vertical tangents at $\pm1$, which no parabola can match.

Guided exampleNotes p. 37

Use the Trapezoid Rule with $n=4$ to approximate $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx$.

Step 1

$\Delta x=\frac{1-(-1)}{4}=\frac12.$
Step 2
The grid points $x_0,\dots,x_4$ are…
$x_i=-1+i\cdot\tfrac12$ for $i=0,\dots,4$. That's 5 points for 4 subintervals.
Step 3
The Trapezoid coefficients for $n=4$ are…
Endpoints ×1 and interior points ×2, with prefactor $\frac{\Delta x}{2}=\frac14$.
Step 4

$\sqrt{\tfrac34}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\approx0.8660$. By symmetry $f(\tfrac12)$ is the same, $f(\pm1)=0$, and $f(0)=1$.
Step 5

$\tfrac14\Big[0+\sqrt3+2+\sqrt3+0\Big]=\tfrac{2+2\sqrt3}{4}=\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\approx1.3660.$
⚠ Heads-up: your notes differ here

Notes p. 37 writes this result as $\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt{15}}{4}\approx1.468$. With $f(\pm\tfrac12)=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, the Trapezoid Rule gives $\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\approx1.366$. This was checked independently with Wolfram Language and Python, and the textbook's Definition 2.6.3 gives the same value. If your professor's version was intentional, ask about it in class.

Step 6
The exact value is $\frac\pi2\approx1.5708$, half of a unit disk. Why is $T_4$ too small?
$\sqrt{1-x^2}$ bulges above each straight top, so every trapezoid misses a sliver of the semicircle. With a concave-up curve the tops lie above the curve instead, and the rule overestimates.
Takeaway. Write $\Delta x$, then list every point, then write the coefficient pattern, and only then evaluate. The pattern is where most mistakes happen.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Grid points in the Midpoint Rule

Why it happens
The Trapezoid and Simpson rules use grid points, so it becomes a habit.

Prevent it
For the Midpoint Rule, write the midpoints $a+\big(i-\tfrac12\big)\Delta x$ explicitly before evaluating.

Wrong coefficient pattern

Why it happens
Memorized patterns blur together.

Prevent it
Rebuild the pattern from the shapes: shared sides count twice (Trapezoid); pair middles get 4 and shared pair ends get 2 (Simpson).

Wrong prefactor ($\Delta x$ vs. $\frac{\Delta x}{2}$ vs. $\frac{\Delta x}{3}$)

Why it happens
The three rules look alike.

Prevent it
Quick check with $f=1$: every rule must give exactly $b-a$. For example, with Simpson, $n=2$: $\frac{\Delta x}{3}(1+4+1)=2\Delta x=b-a$ ✓.

Miscounting points

Why it happens
$n$ strips vs. $n+1$ grid points.

Prevent it
List $x_0,\dots,x_n$ and count: there should be $n+1$ of them, and $x_n$ should equal $b$.

Evaluating $e^{-x^2}$ incorrectly

Why it happens
Order of operations: $-x^2$ means $-(x^2)$.

Prevent it
Write $e^{-(1/4)^2}=e^{-1/16}$, exactly as the notes do on p. 35.

Rounding too early

Why it happens
Rounding each term to 2 decimal places compounds the error.

Prevent it
Keep at least 6 digits until the final step (the textbook carries 12).

Explain why (Level 7)

Why are Simpson's coefficients $1,4,2,4,\dots,4,1$, and why must $n$ be even? Explain it so a classmate could rebuild the rule without memorizing it.

A model explanation

Simpson's Rule fits one parabola over each pair of strips. One parabola through three equally spaced points has area $\frac{\Delta x}{3}(y_{\text{left}}+4y_{\text{mid}}+y_{\text{right}})$. Adding the pairs, each pair's middle point gets a 4. Each point where two pairs meet is the right end of one pair and the left end of the next, so it gets $1+1=2$. The two outermost points get 1. Since everything is done in pairs, the number of strips $n$ has to be even.
