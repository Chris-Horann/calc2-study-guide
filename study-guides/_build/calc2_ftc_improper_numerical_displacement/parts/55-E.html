Stage 1 · Learn

The big idea

Big idea

In §3.1 a particle moved along a line, so its position was a single number. In the plane or in space, position is a point, $\vec p(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))$, and velocity is an arrow, $\vec v(t)=(x'(t),y'(t),z'(t))$. The arrow's direction is where the particle is heading, and its length is how fast it's going: the speed.

There are still two different totals. The displacement $\int_a^b\vec v\,dt=\vec p(b)-\vec p(a)$ is a vector: the straight arrow from start to finish (“This is a vector!”, new notes p. 5). The distance traveled $\int_a^b|\vec v|\,dt$ is a non-negative number: what an odometer would read. The straight arrow can never be longer than the path, so $\big|\int\vec v\,dt\big|\le\int|\vec v|\,dt$.

An everyday version

A runner does one lap of a 400 m track. The displacement is the zero vector, since she's back where she started. The distance traveled is 400 m. A spiral staircase is the helix from new notes p. 6: you rise straight up a little (displacement) while walking around a lot (distance).

Three questions to ask every time
  1. Vector or number? Displacement, “shift in position”: a vector. Distance, “how far”: a number ≥ 0.
  2. Did I differentiate every component, with the chain rule? ($\cos4t\to-4\sin4t$.)
  3. Does the speed simplify before I integrate? Look for $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$ or a perfect square.

Why the two formulas work

  • Displacement: the FTC on each component. $\int_a^bx'(t)\,dt=x(b)-x(a)$, and the same for $y$ and $z$. Put together: $\int_a^b\vec v\,dt=\vec p(b)-\vec p(a)$ (Proposition 3.3.1).
  • Distance: over a tiny time $dt$ the particle moves about (speed) × (time) $=|\vec v|\,dt$. Adding up all those little stretches gives $\int_a^b|\vec v|\,dt$ (Proposition 3.3.3).
  • 1D is the special case. On a line, $|\vec v|=\sqrt{v^2}=|v|$: the §3.1 formula. In 2D and 3D the square root is already ≥ 0, so you never split just to fix signs. If the speed simplifies to an absolute value, like $|2t-2|$ for motion along a line, you split only to integrate that $|\cdot|$, exactly as in §3.1.
  • The inequality $\big|\int\vec v\,dt\big|\le\int|\vec v|\,dt$ says a path can't be shorter than the straight segment joining its ends. They're equal only when the particle moves in one fixed direction.

Tempting alternative, and why to reject it

“Distance $=\big|\vec p(b)-\vec p(a)\big|$.” That's the straight-line shortcut between the endpoints, the length of the displacement. The path is at least that long, and usually longer. For the helix the shortcut is $6\pi$ while the distance is $10\pi$.

Common mistakes: why they happen and how to prevent them

Answering displacement with a number, or distance with a vector

Why it happens
In 1D both were numbers.

Prevent it
New notes p. 5 labels them: displacement “This is a vector!”, distance “This is a non-negative number!”.

Speed as $x'+y'+z'$

Why it happens
Adding feels natural when you're combining components.

Prevent it
Speed is a length: $\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2+z'^2}$ (Pythagoras). Speed is never negative, which a sum can be.

Dropping the chain rule in a component

Why it happens
There are three derivatives to take, and the inside factor is easy to lose in one of them.

Prevent it
Write each component's derivative on its own line. $\cos4t\to-4\sin4t$ and $8t^{3/2}\to12t^{1/2}$.

Using $|\vec p(b)-\vec p(a)|$ as the distance

Why it happens
It's quick, and it's a genuine length.

Prevent it
It's the shortcut's length, a lower bound for the distance. Use it only as a check: distance ≥ it.

Splitting at sign changes in 2D or 3D

Why it happens
§3.1 trained you to split where $v=0$.

Prevent it
In 2D and 3D, $|\vec v|=\sqrt{\cdots}$ is already ≥ 0 everywhere, so just integrate it. You split only if the simplified speed is itself an absolute value, like $|2t-2|$. That's the 1D split, built in because $\sqrt{v^2}=|v|$.

Explain why (Level 7)

In your own words: why is displacement a vector but distance a number, and why can the distance never be smaller than the length of the displacement?

A model explanation

Displacement integrates the velocity vector component by component, and by the FTC each component gives the change in that coordinate. So the result is the arrow $\vec p(b)-\vec p(a)$ from start to finish, which has a direction. Distance integrates the speed $|\vec v|$, a non-negative number: each little stretch of motion adds its length $|\vec v|\,dt$ and nothing cancels. The displacement's length is the straight-line distance between the endpoints, and any path joining two points is at least as long as the straight segment. So $\big|\int\vec v\,dt\big|\le\int|\vec v|\,dt$, with equality only for motion along a line in one direction.
